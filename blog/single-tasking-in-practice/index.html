Single-Tasking in Practice: One Thing on the Screen, One Thing in Your Head

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

The idea is old; the practice is hard

Everyone agrees that doing one thing at a time sounds sensible. The difficulty is that a normal day does not cooperate. Messages arrive, a colleague appears, a notification lights up the phone, and the thing you were doing becomes the thing you were doing before.

This article is about the practice: what you can actually change in a day, and how much research stands behind it. We will be plain about the limits, because the research on multitasking is more mixed than the headlines.

What studies say about switching

In a set of four lab experiments, people either alternated between tasks or repeated the same task. Alternating cost time, and the cost grew when the rules of the tasks were more complex. A cue that told people which task came next reduced the cost. The tasks were simple lab tasks, so the study shows that switching has a price, and what makes the price higher or lower. It does not tell you the price of checking a message at your desk.

Another line of work looks at what an unfinished task leaves behind. Two experiments on attention residue suggest that people need to release a previous task before fully engaging with a new one, and that an unfinished task holds on to attention. In four later studies, a brief plan for how and when to return to an interrupted task reduced that effect.

For the practice, the useful point is the second one: the way you leave a task may be as important as the way you start the next.

What heavy multitasking goes with

A well-known study compared habitual heavy media multitaskers with light ones. The heavy group was more easily distracted by irrelevant information, and did worse on a task-switching test. A later review put this in context: heavier media multitaskers often performed worse across several cognitive areas, many studies found no difference, and the review said that it is still unclear whether multitasking causes the differences or whether people who struggle with focus multitask more.

That distinction matters. If you multitask a lot and find focus hard, this research does not say you broke your attention. It says the two go together, and that the direction is unknown.

Five small ways to try single-tasking

None of these is a tested method. They are ways to put the research findings into a day.

  1. One thing on the screen. Put the current task in front of you as a single line, on paper or on a screen. If it does not fit one line, it is a project, so pick the next small step.
  2. Close the rest, not everything. Close the tabs and windows you will not use for the next block. Leave the ones you need. A cleared screen has fewer prompts to switch.
  3. Park the stray thought. When you remember something else, write it on a list and go back. The list is your promise that it will be handled.
  4. Leave a note when you stop. Write one line: where you are, what comes next. That is a ready-to-resume plan, adapted from the research.
  5. Batch the small stuff. Messages, quick questions and admin go into a slot of their own. A trial with 237 people found that notifications delivered three times a day went with feeling more attentive, in a better mood and more in control than notifications as usual. That study is about phone notifications and used a specific schedule, so it is a hint, not a rule.

What to expect

On the first day, single-tasking often feels slower. That is normal, and it is not data about whether it works. The honest test is how a block feels at the end: did you finish the one thing, and did you feel less scattered? If yes, repeat. If not, adjust the block length or what you remove. There is no number to hit.

FAQ

Is multitasking always bad?

That is not what the research above tests. It is about tasks that both need your attention, and even there the findings on heavy multitaskers are mixed.

Does single-tasking make you more productive?

We found no study that tests single-tasking as a method for productivity in daily work. The switching-cost studies suggest why it might, and that is as far as they go.

What if my job needs me to respond fast?

Then protect shorter blocks and agree on how people can reach you. Even 20 minutes of one thing is a start.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Rubinstein, J. S., Meyer, D. E., & Evans, J. E. (2001). Executive control of cognitive processes in task switching. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 27(4), 763-797. DOI: 10.1037/0096-1523.27.4.763. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11518143/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: four experiments; task alternation produced switching-time costs, larger with complex rules and smaller with cues. What it does not show: sample sizes (not in the abstract), or real-world costs.
  2. Leroy, S. (2009). Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 109(2), 168-181. DOI: 10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002. Read: abstract (RePEc page), summarised on opening. Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: in two experiments, an unfinished task left attention behind. What it does not show: sample size (not in the abstract).
  3. Leroy, S., & Glomb, T. M. (2018). Tasks interrupted: How anticipating time pressure on resumption of an interrupted task causes attention residue and low performance on interrupting tasks and how a "ready-to-resume" plan mitigates the effects. Organization Science, 29, 380-397. DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2017.1184. Read: abstract (Semantic Scholar record). Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: across four studies, a ready-to-resume plan reduced attention residue. What it does not show: sample sizes (not in the abstract).
  4. Ophir, E., Nass, C., & Wagner, A. D. (2009). Cognitive control in media multitaskers. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 106(37), 15583-15587. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.0903620106. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19706386/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: heavy media multitaskers were more susceptible to interference and worse at task switching. What it does not show: cause and effect.
  5. Uncapher, M. R., & Wagner, A. D. (2018). Minds and brains of media multitaskers: Current findings and future directions. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 115(40), 9889-9896. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1611612115. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30275312/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a review with mixed findings, many studies finding no differences. What it does not show: whether multitasking causes the differences.
  6. Fitz, N., Kushlev, K., Jagannathan, R., Lewis, T., Paliwal, D., & Ariely, D. (2019). Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being. Computers in Human Behavior, 101, 84-94. DOI: 10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016. Read: full text (open PDF copy). Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: in a randomised field experiment (N = 237, two weeks), batching three times a day went with feeling more attentive, in a better mood and more in control. What it does not show: effects beyond two weeks or for other groups; outcomes were mostly self-reported.
