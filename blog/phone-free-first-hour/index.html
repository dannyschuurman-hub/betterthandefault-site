The Phone-Free First Hour: What Is Known and What Is Just a Good Idea

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

The advice everyone has heard

"Do not touch your phone for the first hour." It is easy to say, and it sounds right. The first minutes of a day feel like they belong to you, and a feed or an inbox hands them to someone else before you are properly awake.

We found no study that tested a phone-free first hour, so this article cannot say the habit works. Related research looks at task switching and at what prompts people to scroll. We say what it does and does not tell us, and suggest a way to try the habit that costs nothing.

Evidence that is close, but not the same

Unfinished business stays in your head. Two lines of work on what researchers call attention residue, from an experimental paper and a four-study follow-up, suggest that when you leave a task unfinished, part of your attention stays with it, and your next task suffers. The follow-up found that briefly planning your return to an interrupted task, a "ready-to-resume" plan, reduced the effect. A morning feed or inbox is full of open loops, so it is a fair guess that opening it first fills your head with half-started things. That is our reasoning, not a finding about mornings.

Why we reach for the phone. In a seven-day social media break with 61 people, interviews pointed to habit, coping and boredom as motives for scrolling, and to notifications as a trigger. That is a before-and-after study with no control group, and the interviews are descriptive. In that study, people named habit, coping, boredom and notifications as reasons for scrolling.

What we could not use. One study on checking email less often looked relevant, but we could only reach a news summary of it, not the paper or its abstract, so we left it out. That is the rule we follow for every article: if we have not opened the source, it does not go in. It also means this article has fewer pillars than you might expect, and we would rather say so.

What this adds up to

Two indirect findings: unfinished tasks seem to cost attention, and phone use is often driven by habit and notifications. Neither tests the first hour of the day. None says that an hour is the right length, or that morning is the right time. A phone-free first hour is a sensible bet built on these, not a proven method.

There is also a practical point the studies do not cover. Many people use their phone as an alarm, for the weather, for a message from a child or a parent. Make room for the things you need your phone for in the morning.

A way to try it that bends

  1. Decide what the phone is for in the morning. Alarm only, or alarm plus one thing you need, for example the day's calendar.
  2. Charge it across the room. This is a habit step, not a tested one. If the phone is not within reach in bed, the first minute is easier.
  3. Write down one task for the day before you open anything. On paper, or in a note you wrote the night before. This is our adaptation of the ready-to-resume idea: you start the day with your own list, not someone else's.
  4. Pick a start time that is realistic. Fifteen minutes still counts. The aim is to notice what the morning feels like without the feed, not to win.
  5. Look at the result after a week. Did the first hour feel different? If not, you have learned something, and you can drop it.

Keep the people who may need you in an emergency able to reach you. That setup was not tested either, so it stays our advice.

FAQ

Is there proof that a phone-free morning helps?

No. We found no study of this exact habit. The related studies are about unfinished tasks and the habits behind scrolling.

How long should the phone-free time be?

There is no tested length. Choose something you can keep, such as 15 or 30 minutes, and extend it only if you want to.

What if I need my phone as an alarm?

Use it as an alarm. If you need to check your calendar or stay reachable, decide that in advance. The point is not to avoid the device but to avoid the feed and the inbox first thing. This article gives no medical advice, and nothing here is a promise.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Leroy, S. (2009). Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 109(2), 168-181. DOI: 10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002. Read: abstract (RePEc page), summarised on opening. Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: in two experiments, an unfinished earlier task left attention behind and made the next task harder. What it does not show: sample size (not in the abstract), or effects of a morning feed.
  2. Leroy, S., & Glomb, T. M. (2018). Tasks interrupted: How anticipating time pressure on resumption of an interrupted task causes attention residue and low performance on interrupting tasks and how a "ready-to-resume" plan mitigates the effects. Organization Science, 29, 380-397. DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2017.1184. Read: abstract (Semantic Scholar record). Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: across four studies, a short ready-to-resume plan reduced attention residue. What it does not show: sample sizes (not in the abstract), or any effect on morning routines.
  3. Brown, L., & Kuss, D. J. (2020). Fear of missing out, mental wellbeing, and social connectedness: A seven-day social media abstinence trial. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 17(12), 4566. DOI: 10.3390/ijerph17124566. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32599962/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in 61 people, interviews pointed to coping, habit and boredom as motives for scrolling and to notifications as a trigger. What it does not show: a controlled comparison; there was no control group.
