Attention Residue: Why Switching Tasks Leaves Your Mind Behind

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

The feeling you already know

You finish reading a message, go back to your report, and for a few minutes the report does not quite come back. Your eyes are on the page, but part of you is still in that message. Researchers have a name for this: attention residue. It describes thoughts about a previous task that linger while you try to work on a new one.

It sounds small. But modern work, and modern phones, are built out of switches. A chat window, an email, a notification, a calendar alert, then back to the task. If each switch leaves a bit of residue, a day with many switches is a day with a lot of leftover thinking.

This article looks at what has actually been tested, and it is careful about the limits. Most of this research is about work tasks in controlled experiments, not about phones in particular.

What the research found

Stopping the old task is the hard part. In a pair of experiments on switching between work tasks, people had to stop thinking about a previous task before they could fully focus on a new one. When they did not manage that, their performance on the next task suffered. Finishing the first task was not enough for a clean transition by itself. The abstract does not say how many people took part.

Time pressure on the old task can make it worse or better, depending. The same research reported that time pressure during the first task helped people let go of it, which in turn improved their performance on the next task. A later set of four studies looked at the opposite case: when people expected to go back to an interrupted task under time pressure, they found it harder to focus on the interrupting task, showing more attention residue and weaker performance on it. A short "ready-to-resume" step, in which people briefly reflected on and planned their return to the interrupted task, reduced that effect.

A notification can be enough. In a separate set of experiments, receiving a phone notification alone, without touching the phone, disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task. The size of the disruption was comparable to that of actively using the phone for a call or a text message. The authors link this to task-irrelevant thoughts, or mind wandering, that a notification prompts. The abstract does not give the sample size.

What this does not show

It does not show that every switch costs you a fixed number of minutes. We have not read a source we can quote for a number of minutes, so we will not give you one.

It does not show that the effect is the same for every task, every person or every phone. The attention residue studies were about work tasks and time pressure. The notification study used an attention-demanding task in a lab. Neither tested a person at a real desk, over a real week, with a real phone.

And it does not prove that putting your phone out of sight helps. That is a sensible idea, but we have not read a study that supports it, so we keep it as advice.

Three small ways to cut the residue

  1. Close the loop before you switch. If you must leave a task, write one line about where you were and what comes next. This is the spirit of the ready-to-resume idea: a short plan to return, so your mind has less reason to keep rehearsing it.
  2. Batch the interruptions. If messages come in all day, each one is a possible switch. Fewer, planned moments of checking mean fewer switches. (We cover notification batches in a separate article.)
  3. Work in a block with one task. Choose one thing, give it a set time, and let everything else wait until the block ends.

FAQ

What is attention residue?

It is the effect where thoughts about a previous task stay active while you try to work on a new one, which can make the new task harder to do well. The term comes from research on switching between work tasks.

Does a phone notification really disrupt attention?

In one set of experiments, a notification alone, without interacting with the phone, disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task by an amount similar to using the phone. That is a lab result, so treat it as a sign, not as a rule for your day.

How long does it take to refocus after a switch?

We do not give a number. None of the sources we read and can cite gives one, and a made-up figure would be worse than none.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Leroy, S. (2009). Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 109(2), 168-181. DOI: 10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002 (DOI taken from the Crossref record; the record page at ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v109y2009i2p168-181.html was opened and read; the publisher page is subscriber-only). Not listed in PubMed. Read: abstract, paraphrased. What it shows: in two experiments, people who did not stop thinking about a previous task performed worse on the next one, and time pressure on the first task helped them disengage. What it does not show: the abstract gives no sample size, and the tasks were experimental work tasks.
  2. Leroy, S., & Glomb, T. M. (2018). Tasks interrupted: How anticipating time pressure on resumption of an interrupted task causes attention residue and low performance on interrupting tasks and how a "ready-to-resume" plan mitigates the effects. Organization Science. DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2017.1184. Read: abstract (via the Semantic Scholar record). What it shows: across four studies, anticipated time pressure on resuming an interrupted task went with attention residue and weaker performance on the interrupting task, and a brief ready-to-resume plan reduced it. What it does not show: sample sizes (not in the abstract) or effects in everyday phone use.
  3. Stothart, C., Mitchum, A., & Yehnert, C. (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance. DOI: 10.1037/xhp0000100. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121498/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: notifications alone disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task, by an amount comparable to active phone use. What it does not show: the abstract gives no sample size, and it is a lab task, not real-life work.
