Doomscrolling and the Feed: What Helps and What We Cannot Say

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

What people mean by doomscrolling

Doomscrolling is the habit of scrolling through a feed long after you meant to stop, often through content that leaves you feeling worse. It is a popular word, not a medical one, and we will use it that way: a name for a habit, nothing more.

A feed has no natural end. There is no last page, no final chapter, no moment where the content simply runs out. Every swipe brings something new, and that is the whole design. If you have ever looked up and found twenty minutes gone, that is not a character flaw. An endless feed gives you no clear cue to stop.

This article looks at what a few studies say about interrupting that pattern. We do not make claims about brain chemistry, because none of the sources we read measured it.

What the research can tell us

A habit people can describe, measured in questionnaires. One set of three studies, with 378, 419 and 460 people, built a questionnaire for doomscrolling. People who scored higher also tended to score higher on a measure of problematic social media use, on fear of missing out, and on psychological distress, and the link between doomscrolling and well-being measures ran through that distress. This is a snapshot, not an experiment. It shows that these things go together. It does not show that one causes the other.

A small pause before opening an app. In a six-week field experiment with 280 participants, an app that adds a short pause when you try to open a chosen app made people think twice. On average, in 36 percent of their attempts to open a chosen app, they closed it again after the pause. By the sixth week, people attempted to open those apps 37 percent less than in week one, and actual opens were down 57 percent. In a separate online experiment with 500 people, the option to dismiss the attempt had the strongest effect, a short delay also reduced viewing, and a deliberation message on its own did not. The abstract describes no control group for the six-week part, so we cannot rule out that people were simply trying hard because they knew they were in a study.

A daily limit on social apps. In a randomised trial, 143 undergraduates at one university either limited three social platforms to 10 minutes each per day or used them as usual, for three weeks. The limited group had lower scores on a loneliness scale and a depression scale than the control group. Both groups showed lower anxiety and less fear of missing out than at baseline, which the authors suggest may come from simply monitoring your own use. These are students at one university, using three particular platforms, and the app limit itself on an iPhone was not what was tested.

A week without social media. In a seven-day study of 61 people, there was no control group: people were compared before and after. After the week, scores for well-being and social connectedness went up, while fear of missing out and phone use went down. Interviews pointed to coping, habit and boredom as reasons people scroll, and to notifications as a reason it was hard to stay away. Many people filled the time with other apps.

What to take from this

Two of these studies tested ways to change social media use: a short pause before opening an app, and a daily time limit. Both have the limits described above. None of this is a cure, and none of it proves that a particular app or setting will work for you. Removing an icon from your home screen was not tested in any of the studies we read.

One more point from the pause study: the strongest single ingredient was giving people a clear way out. When you are about to open a feed, a moment where you can say "no, not now" seems to matter more than a message telling you to reflect.

Three small things to try

  1. Move the app that eats your time off your home screen. Press and hold the icon, choose Remove App, then Remove from Home Screen. It stays in your App Library, one search away. That one extra step is the point.
  2. Set a short daily limit for social apps: Settings, Screen Time, App Limits, Add Limit, then Social.
  3. When you notice yourself scrolling, ask what you were looking for. If you cannot answer, that is your cue to stop.

FAQ

Is doomscrolling an addiction?

This article cannot answer that and is not medical advice. The research we read describes it as a habit measured with questionnaires, and it shows associations with distress, not a diagnosis. If your phone use worries you, talk to a professional.

Does a dopamine detox work?

None of the studies we read measured dopamine, so we make no claim either way. The studies above tested a pause before opening an app and a daily limit on social apps, and each is described with its limits.

Will an app limit make me less lonely or depressed?

One three-week trial with undergraduates found lower scores on those scales with a 10-minute-per-platform limit, compared with usual use. That is one study, with students, and it does not promise anything for you.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Satici, S. A., Gocet Tekin, E., Deniz, M. E., & Satici, B. (2023). Doomscrolling scale: Its association with personality traits, psychological distress, social media use, and wellbeing. Applied Research in Quality of Life. DOI: 10.1007/s11482-022-10110-7. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36275044/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a doomscrolling questionnaire built across three studies (N = 378, 419, 460), linked to distress, fear of missing out and well-being measures. What it does not show: causation; all three studies are questionnaire snapshots.
  2. Grüning, D. J., Riedel, F., & Lorenz-Spreen, P. (2023). Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2213114120. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36795756/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in a six-week field experiment (N = 280), app openings fell 57 percent after six weeks; in an online experiment (N = 500), the option to dismiss mattered most. What it does not show: effects against a control group in the field part (none described in the abstract), or effects beyond six weeks. (The app's name appears only in the paper's own title.)
  3. Hunt, M. G., Marx, R., Lipson, C., & Young, J. (2018). No more FOMO: Limiting social media decreases loneliness and depression. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology. DOI: 10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751. Read: abstract (via the Semantic Scholar record; the publisher page refused automated access). Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: in a randomised trial of 143 undergraduates over three weeks, limiting three platforms to 10 minutes each per day lowered loneliness and depression scores versus usual use. What it does not show: results for non-students, longer periods, or other apps.
  4. Brown, L., & Kuss, D. J. (2020). Fear of missing out, mental wellbeing, and social connectedness: A seven-day social media abstinence trial. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 17(12), 4566. DOI: 10.3390/ijerph17124566. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32599962/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: with 61 people before and after seven days without social media, well-being and connectedness rose and phone use fell. What it does not show: whether a control group would have looked the same; the design had none.
