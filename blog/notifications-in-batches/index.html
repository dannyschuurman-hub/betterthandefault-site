Notifications in Batches: What Happened When People Got Them Three Times a Day

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

Two obvious fixes, and a third

When your phone pings too much, there are two obvious fixes. You can leave things as they are and put up with it. Or you can switch notifications off entirely and check when you feel like it.

There is a third option that gets less attention: keep the notifications, but have them arrive in a few planned batches a day. Researchers tested whether getting notifications at fixed times changed how people felt.

The trial

The study was a randomised field experiment with 237 participants. The people were smartphone owners living in India, on average about 30 years old, recruited through an online worker panel. They used an app built by the researchers that controlled how their notifications were delivered. For two weeks, participants were randomly assigned to one of several conditions: notifications as usual, notifications in an hourly batch, notifications in a batch three times a day (at 9 am, 3 pm and 9 pm), or no notifications at all. (Checked against the full text on 24 November: the hourly condition did not differ from the control, apart from feeling less interrupted.) Each day they filled in short surveys about how they felt.

What it found

Compared with the group that received notifications as usual, people whose notifications were batched three times a day felt more attentive, were in a better mood, and felt in greater control of their phones. The no-notifications group did not get most of those benefits, and reported higher levels of anxiety and fear of missing out. The authors report that inattention and phone-related fear of missing out helped explain these results.

Put simply: in this trial, people who received no notifications reported more anxiety and fear of missing out, while people who received three batches a day reported benefits on several measures compared with notifications as usual. One possible reading, which is ours and not the authors: if you know the next batch is coming at a set time, you may worry less about what you are missing.

What it does not show

It is one trial, and it has real limits. The authors point out that the treatment lasted only two weeks, so we do not know what happens after the novelty fades. The sample was a particular group of people in one country, recruited online, and most of the outcomes were what people said about themselves in daily surveys. People in the trial also lived among others who still expected them to reply quickly, which may have changed how it felt.

The trial also tested a very specific schedule. We do not know whether 9, 13 and 18 would work as well as 9, 15 and 21, or whether three batches is better than four. It is a good sign for the idea of batching. It is not a rule about the exact hours.

Why a notification costs something

A second line of evidence helps explain why this could matter. In a set of experiments, receiving a phone notification alone, without touching the phone, disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task. The size of the effect was comparable to that of actively using the phone for a call or text. The authors connect this to task-irrelevant thoughts that a notification can prompt. The abstract does not give the sample size, and the task was a lab task, so it is a hint, not a measure of your workday.

A third study, the seven-day social media break with 61 people, found something similar from the other side. In interviews, people said notifications made it hard to stay away from social media and triggered fear of missing out. That study had no control group, and the finding is qualitative.

How to set it up on an iPhone

  1. Open Settings, then Notifications, then Scheduled Summary.
  2. Turn it on and pick the apps that should wait for the summary.
  3. Choose your times. The trial used 9 am, 3 pm and 9 pm, but you can pick times that fit your day, for example morning, midday and early evening.
  4. Keep important people outside the summary. Messages and calls from people you care about can still come through as they happen.

Notice step four. In the trial, nobody sorted notifications by who they came from, so that part is our own idea, not a tested one.

FAQ

Should I turn off all notifications?

In this trial, turning them off completely did not bring most of the benefits of batching, and it went with more anxiety and fear of missing out. That is one trial with a specific group, so treat it as a hint that batching may be worth trying before you switch everything off.

Which three times should I choose?

The study used 9 am, 3 pm and 9 pm. Choose times that suit your day. The key idea is that the times are regular and few.

Does batching notifications make me more productive?

The trial reported that people felt more attentive and in a better mood, and in greater control. It was mostly based on self-reported feelings, so we would not promise more output.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Fitz, N., Kushlev, K., Jagannathan, R., Lewis, T., Paliwal, D., & Ariely, D. (2019). Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being. Computers in Human Behavior, 101, 84-94. DOI: 10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016. Read: full text (open PDF copy) and abstract. Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: in a randomised field experiment (N = 237, two weeks), notifications batched three times a day went with feeling more attentive, in a better mood and more in control; no notifications went with higher anxiety and fear of missing out. What it does not show: effects beyond two weeks, effects for other groups or schedules, or objective outcomes; most outcome measures were self-reported.
  2. Stothart, C., Mitchum, A., & Yehnert, C. (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance. DOI: 10.1037/xhp0000100. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121498/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: notifications alone disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task. What it does not show: sample size (not in the abstract), or real-life effects.
  3. Brown, L., & Kuss, D. J. (2020). Fear of missing out, mental wellbeing, and social connectedness: A seven-day social media abstinence trial. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 17(12), 4566. DOI: 10.3390/ijerph17124566. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32599962/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: interviews in a 61-person study pointed to notifications as a challenge to staying away and as a trigger of fear of missing out. What it does not show: a controlled comparison; there was no control group.
