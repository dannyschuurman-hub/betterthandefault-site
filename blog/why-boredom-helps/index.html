Why Boredom Might Help: What the Research Does and Does Not Say

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

The gap we fill without noticing

A queue, a lift, a dull minute before a meeting. Not long ago, those moments were empty. Now most of us fill them within a second, usually with a phone. It is so automatic that you may not notice it happening.

The usual case for boredom is that it is a signal rather than a problem, and that a little of it might be good for you. We want to be careful here, because "boredom is good for you" is the kind of claim that gets repeated far past what the evidence supports. So this article looks at what a few studies found, and where they stop.

What the studies found

Dull tasks, then more creative answers. In two studies by the same authors, one with 80 participants and one with 90, people first did a boring activity, such as a dull writing task or a dull reading task, and then did a creative task. The authors report that the boring activities led to more creative performance, and that in some circumstances, such as convergent tasks, the boring reading task led to more creativity than the boring writing task. The authors discuss daydreaming as one possible link between boredom and creativity. These were short lab tasks. The study does not show that a long commute or an hour of scrolling makes you more creative.

Most of us do not enjoy being alone with our thoughts. In a series of 11 studies, participants spent 6 to 15 minutes in a room by themselves with nothing to do except think. Most did not enjoy it and preferred doing mundane external activities. According to the abstract, many participants preferred to give themselves electric shocks rather than be left alone with their thoughts; it does not say in how many of the 11 studies this happened. Read that carefully: it says unstructured time is hard for people, and it says nothing about whether it is good for you. The abstract does not give the sample sizes for the individual studies.

Boredom as a signal. One review proposed a model in which boredom is a signal that your attention is not engaged in something you value. In this model, boredom has two parts: a mismatch between what a task demands and what your mind has available, and a mismatch between an activity and your goals, or a lack of goals at all. The model also says boredom can come from too little stimulation and from too much. It is a theory, supported by the authors' own empirical work, and the abstract is careful to call it a model, not a fact.

Boredom and scrolling. In a seven-day social media break study of 61 people, interviews pointed to boredom as one of the motives for using social media, along with coping and habit, and many people filled their time with other apps. This is a qualitative finding from a small study. It fits the idea that the phone is the easy answer to an empty moment.

What you can fairly take from this

Here is a cautious reading. Boredom is not a defect to be fixed in two seconds. It may be a useful signal that you want something more meaningful, and in lab tasks a dull stretch was followed by more creative answers. At the same time, plenty of people find empty time unpleasant, and that is normal. None of the studies we read showed that being bored on purpose will make you creative, happier or better at your job.

What we can say is smaller and more practical: if the phone fills every gap, you never find out what the gap would have been. Trying a few empty moments on purpose is a low-cost experiment, and you can stop whenever you like.

Three small ways to leave a gap

  1. Leave the phone in the bag in one familiar queue. Pick a single place, such as the shop or the lift, and see what your mind does.
  2. Take real breaks. After a focus sprint, stand up, look outside or step outside. In one experiment with 308 employees, a break with nature exposure gave better recovery than a break with short videos.
  3. Ask what the boredom is about. If you are bored with a task, the review above would say the task may lack meaning for you, or demand too little or too much. That is useful to know, and it is not the phone's problem.

FAQ

Does boredom make you more creative?

In two lab studies, the authors report that doing a boring activity first was followed by more creative performance. That is a small, specific result, not a promise that boredom will make you creative.

Is it bad to use my phone when I am bored?

No. This article is not saying that. It suggests that if you always do it, you may miss the signal that boredom gives you. Using your phone is a normal choice.

Why do I find it so hard to just sit and think?

In 11 studies, most participants did not enjoy 6 to 15 minutes alone with their thoughts. So you are in good company. It does not mean something is wrong with you.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Mann, S., & Cadman, R. (2014). Does being bored make us more creative? Creativity Research Journal, 26(2), 165-173. DOI: 10.1080/10400419.2014.901073. Read: abstract and record page, paraphrased (knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/22263); the publisher page was not opened. Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: two studies (N = 80 and N = 90) in which boring activities led to more creative performance, with differences between reading and writing tasks. What it does not show: effects outside short lab tasks, or the mechanism (daydreaming is only discussed as a possibility).
  2. Wilson, T. D., Reinhard, D. A., Westgate, E. C., Gilbert, D. T., Ellerbeck, N., Hahn, C., Brown, C. L., & Shaked, A. (2014). Just think: The challenges of the disengaged mind. Science. DOI: 10.1126/science.1250830. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24994650/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in 11 studies, people typically did not enjoy 6 to 15 minutes alone with their thoughts, and many preferred an unpleasant activity to doing nothing. What it does not show: whether time alone with your thoughts is good for you.
  3. Westgate, E. C., & Wilson, T. D. (2018). Boring thoughts and bored minds: The MAC model of boredom and cognitive engagement. Psychological Review. DOI: 10.1037/rev0000097. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29963873/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a model in which boredom reflects unsuccessful attentional engagement in valued, goal-congruent activity, with separate attention and meaning components. What it does not show: that boredom is beneficial; it is a theoretical account with supporting evidence.
  4. Brown, L., & Kuss, D. J. (2020). Fear of missing out, mental wellbeing, and social connectedness: A seven-day social media abstinence trial. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 17(12), 4566. DOI: 10.3390/ijerph17124566. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32599962/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: interviews in a 61-person study named boredom, coping and habit as motives for social media use. What it does not show: how common this is beyond this sample; there was no control group.
  5. Grobelny, J., Glinka, M., & Chirkowska-Smolak, T. (2024). The impact of hedonic social media use during microbreaks on employee resources recovery. Scientific Reports. DOI: 10.1038/s41598-024-72825-x. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39284901/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in 308 employees, nature exposure gave better recovery than algorithmic video clips in a microbreak. What it does not show: that boredom itself helps, because this study compared break types, not boredom.

The wider idea that empty moments are worth having, and that boredom is useful, is our advice and nothing more: Our advice, not proven.
