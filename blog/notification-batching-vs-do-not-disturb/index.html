Notification Batching vs. Do Not Disturb: Which One Fits You?

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

Two ways to quiet a phone

Two iPhone settings can help you cut down on interruptions. Do Not Disturb silences alerts while it is active, subject to the exceptions you set. Scheduled Summary collects notifications from the apps you select and delivers them at times you choose, for example three times a day.

They sound like the same idea, but they ask different things of you. With Do Not Disturb you give up your phone's alerts for a stretch. With batching you keep them, but on a rhythm. Which one fits depends on what you are trying to protect, and on who expects to reach you.

What a full day without notifications felt like

One small study asked 30 volunteers to turn off notifications on all their devices for 24 hours and compared that with a baseline day. This is the closest thing we found to a test of "silence everything". Without notifications, participants felt less distracted and more productive.

That is the appealing half. The other half is just as clear in the abstract: some participants felt less able to respond as quickly as people expected, which made them anxious, and they felt less connected to their social groups. Participants also reported plans to change how they handle notifications, and about half said they were still following through two years later.

Two cautions. The version we read is a preprint, not the peer-reviewed conference version. And the study ran for only one day, with 30 people who volunteered, so it says little about what a month of silence feels like.

What batching three times a day felt like

A randomised field experiment with 237 smartphone owners compared several conditions over two weeks, including notifications as usual, notifications delivered three times a day, and no notifications. People in the batching condition reported feeling more attentive, in a better mood and more in control. The no-notifications group reported more anxiety and more fear of missing out. We describe this trial in more detail in our article on notifications in batches.

Put the two studies side by side and a pattern shows up, though it is a loose one. In the one-day study, people reported less distraction, and some worried about replying slowly. In the batching trial, people who received no notifications reported more anxiety and fear of missing out, and people who received three batches a day reported benefits on several self-reported measures compared with notifications as usual.

Why the comparison is loose

These are different studies, with different people, different lengths and different questions. One lasted a day, the other two weeks. One recruited 30 volunteers, the other randomised 237 people into conditions. None of the studies we read ran both settings against each other in the same group. So we cannot say that batching beats Do Not Disturb. We can say that in the one-day study some volunteers worried about slow replies, and that in the batching trial the group that received no notifications reported more anxiety and fear of missing out than the group that received notifications as usual.

A related lab result explains why people reach for silence at all. In an experiment on an attention-demanding task, a notification alone disrupted performance, even when people did not touch the phone. That was a lab task, so it does not tell us how much a notification costs in a normal workday. It does explain the instinct.

A simple way to choose

If your problem is a stretch of work that must not break, use Do Not Disturb for that stretch only, and let people who may need you in an emergency through.

If your problem is a day full of small pings, try batching. Open Settings, then Notifications, then Scheduled Summary, pick the apps that should wait, and choose your times. The trial tested three daily batches at 9 am, 3 pm and 9 pm, as well as hourly batches; the hourly batches did not differ from usual notifications on most measures. If you try three batches a day, choose times that suit you.

Either way, check which calls and messages can still come through with that setting. Messages and calls from the few people who really need you should still arrive. The studies did not test that exact setup, so it is our advice, not a result.

FAQ

Is Do Not Disturb better than batching?

We cannot say. The studies we read tested them separately, with different people and lengths of time. Going without notifications for a day gave less distraction and some worry about being unreachable in a one-day study. Batching did well in a two-week trial on self-reported attention and mood.

Will I miss something important?

That is the worry both studies touch on. Letting a short list of people through is a common approach, but this exact step was not tested in these studies.

How long should I try it?

There is no tested answer. A week is long enough to see whether the rhythm suits you. If it makes you more anxious, that is useful information, and you can change it.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Pielot, M., & Rello, L. (2017). Productive, anxious, lonely: 24 hours without push notifications. arXiv preprint, version 3 (first posted 2016). DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.1612.02314 (the conference version has DOI 10.1145/3098279.3098526; not opened). Read: abstract page on arXiv, summarised on opening. What it shows: with notifications off for 24 hours, 30 volunteers felt less distracted and more productive, but some felt anxious about slow replies and less connected; about half were still following through two years later. What it does not show: effects beyond one day, effects in a controlled comparison with batching, or anything about the peer-reviewed version.
  2. Fitz, N., Kushlev, K., Jagannathan, R., Lewis, T., Paliwal, D., & Ariely, D. (2019). Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being. Computers in Human Behavior, 101, 84-94. DOI: 10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016. Read: full text (open PDF copy). Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: in a randomised field experiment (N = 237, two weeks), notifications batched three times a day went with feeling more attentive, in a better mood and more in control; no notifications went with higher anxiety and fear of missing out. What it does not show: effects beyond two weeks, other schedules, or objective outcomes.
  3. Stothart, C., Mitchum, A., & Yehnert, C. (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 41(4), 893-897. DOI: 10.1037/xhp0000100. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121498/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: notifications alone disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task. What it does not show: sample size (not in the abstract), or real-life effects.
