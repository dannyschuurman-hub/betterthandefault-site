Screen Time Limits That Work: What the Trials Say About Setting a Cap

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

The limit that you ignore

Almost everyone who has set a daily app limit knows the moment: the screen says time is up, and a tap later you have another 15 minutes. A limit that is easy to dismiss often turns into a reminder. So the useful question is not whether limits exist, but which kinds have shown an effect in a study.

Four trials are worth knowing about. Each is small or short, and each tested something a little different. Taken together they say something about how to set a limit, though not about the perfect number.

A limit on social platforms

In a three-week randomised trial, 143 undergraduates at one university either limited three social platforms to 10 minutes each per day or used them as usual. After a week of baseline monitoring, the limited group showed significant reductions in loneliness and depression scores compared with the control group. Both groups showed decreases in anxiety and fear of missing out compared with baseline, which the authors read as a sign that simply monitoring yourself can help.

This is the study behind the idea of capping social apps. Treat it with care: it was one university, students, three weeks, and the abstract does not say which phone feature, if any, enforced the limit.

A limit you set yourself, compared with a gray screen

In the pre-registered field experiment with 112 participants, people who set their own time limit reduced screen time, but by less than people who switched to grayscale, and the effect was more gradual. Neither group showed an immediate change in well-being or academic performance. That suggests a limit helps, but a quiet one does less than a change to how the phone looks and feels.

A reminder that you can dismiss

A three-week randomised trial of 70 iPhone users tested a customisable app that showed a full-screen reminder whenever a session on a chosen app passed a time limit the user had set, with the option to quit or continue. Daily time on the participant's most problematic app fell by about 29 minutes. But the intervention group did not show a significant reduction in problematic social media use or an increase in self-efficacy. The authors call the efficacy short term.

That gives a useful reading: a limit that pops up and lets you choose can still shave time off the one app that bothers you most, even if it does not change how you feel about your use.

A pause before opening

A six-week field experiment with 280 participants tested an app that added a short pause, with a message and an option to back out, when people tried to open an app they had chosen. On average, 36 percent of the time participants closed the app again after the pause, and over six weeks the number of attempts to open the app fell. The abstract describes no control group for that part. In a separate online experiment with 500 people, the option to dismiss the attempt had the strongest effect, a time delay also helped, and the message alone did not.

What these point to

  • Make the moment of decision visible. Two of the four trials worked by interrupting the opening or the long session with a choice.
  • Pick the app that bothers you. The 29-minute drop was on the participant's most problematic app, not on everything.
  • Keep the limit modest, and start with one platform. Nobody tested the best number. Ten minutes per platform worked in one student trial, which does not make it the right number for you.
  • Do not expect the limit to fix how you feel. Effects on well-being were mixed or absent in the trials that measured them.

How to set a limit on an iPhone

Open Settings, then Screen Time, then App Limits, add a category such as Social, and choose a daily time. Decide beforehand what you will do when it is reached. If you tap through every time, try a lower limit, which you can undo at any moment. The trials did not test Apple's own limits, so what we describe here is a way to apply the idea.

FAQ

How much should my limit be?

No trial tested the best number. One used 10 minutes per platform with students. Start with what is realistic for you, and lower it slowly.

Do limits improve well-being?

Mixed. One three-week trial found lower loneliness and depression scores, others found no well-being change or did not measure it. None of this is medical advice.

What if I keep ignoring the limit?

Then use it as information. The reminder shows how often the habit fires, and one app is easier to change than all of them.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Hunt, M. G., Marx, R., Lipson, C., & Young, J. (2018). No more FOMO: Limiting social media decreases loneliness and depression. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology. DOI: 10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751. Read: abstract (Semantic Scholar record; the publisher page refused automated access). Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: 143 undergraduates, baseline week then three weeks, limiting three platforms to 10 minutes each lowered loneliness and depression scores versus usual use. What it does not show: effects in other groups, or beyond three weeks. Volume and pages not checked, so not given.
  2. Zimmermann, L., & Sobolev, M. (2023). Digital strategies for screen time reduction: A randomized field experiment. Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking, 26(1), 42-49. DOI: 10.1089/cyber.2022.0027. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36577008/. Read: abstract (via PubMed record and Europe PMC). What it shows: time-limit goals reduced screen time less, and more gradually, than grayscale (N = 112). What it does not show: well-being effects.
  3. Mertens, L., Brockmeier, L. C., Roitzheim, C., Radtke, T., Dingler, T., & Keller, J. (2026). JMIR mHealth and uHealth, 14, e56824. DOI: 10.2196/56824. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41950504/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a 3-week randomised trial with 70 iPhone users; about 29 minutes less daily time on the most problematic app; no significant change in problematic social media use or self-efficacy. What it does not show: long-term effects. (Title left out because it contains the app's brand name.)
  4. Grüning, D. J., Riedel, F., & Lorenz-Spreen, P. (2023). Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 120(8), e2213114120. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2213114120. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36795756/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a six-week field experiment (N = 280) and an online experiment (N = 500); the option to dismiss had the strongest effect. What it does not show: a control group in the field part (none described in the abstract). (Title left out because it contains the app's brand name.)
  5. Olson, J. A., Sandra, D. A., Chmoulevitch, D., Raz, A., & Veissiere, S. P. L. (2022). A nudge-based intervention to reduce problematic smartphone use: Randomised controlled trial. International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction, 1-23. DOI: 10.1007/s11469-022-00826-w. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35600564/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a ten-strategy package lowered problematic use and screen time versus a monitoring control (N = 70). What it does not show: the effect of any single strategy.
