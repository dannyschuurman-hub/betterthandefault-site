The Doomscrolling Reset in 7 Days: A Trial You Run on Yourself

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

What this is, and what it is not

This is a plan, not a result. We have not run it on a group of people, and we have no numbers to show you. Nobody has tested this exact seven-day sequence either. We built it from studies on related changes, and we kept it small on purpose.

Think of it as a trial you run on yourself. You collect your own numbers, from Screen Time on your own phone and from a few lines in a notebook, and you decide at the end whether anything changed. If the answer is no, that is a valid result.

What the research gives us to build on

Doomscrolling goes with distress, in surveys. A paper with three studies (N = 378, 419 and 460) built a scale for doomscrolling and found it associated with personality traits, problematic social media use, fear of missing out and some features of social media use, and, through psychological distress, with lower well-being. These are correlational studies. They show that heavy doomscrolling and distress go together, not that one causes the other.

A week away from social media. In a seven-day trial with 61 people, scores for well-being and social connectedness went up and fear of missing out and phone use went down after a week of staying away from social media. There was no control group, so other things that changed that week could play a role. Interviews pointed to coping, habit and boredom as reasons for scrolling, and to notifications as a trigger.

A pause before opening. In a six-week field experiment with 280 participants, an app that added a short pause with an option to back out meant that in 36 percent of their attempts to open a chosen app, people closed it again. The abstract describes no control group for that part.

A package of small nudges. A randomised trial with a control group found that a package of strategies, including disabling non-essential notifications and grayscale, lowered problematic smartphone use and screen time and improved sleep quality.

Nothing here claims that seven days resets anything. The number is a practical choice: long enough to see a pattern, short enough to finish.

The seven days

Day 1 and Day 7 are for measuring. On Days 2 to 6 you add one change a day and keep the earlier ones.

Day 1: Measure. Change nothing. Open Screen Time and write down yesterday's total and the minutes on the one app you scroll most. Write one line: when do you scroll most?

Day 2: Remove the trigger. Turn off notifications for your news and social apps. This follows the notification finding from the week-away study and the nudge package.

Day 3: Add a pause. Move the app off your home screen, or into a folder on the last page, so opening it takes two extra steps. That is our version of the pause idea.

Day 4: Name the reason. The first time you reach for the app today, write the reason in two words: bored, anxious, tired, waiting. No judgement. The week-away interviews suggest habit and boredom are common.

Day 5: Pick a swap. For your most common reason, choose a small alternative: a short walk, water, three slow breaths, a page of a book. Use it once.

Day 6: Set a stopping point. Decide when you will stop scrolling, for example after dinner, or set a 20-minute evening limit. Use App Limits if you like.

Day 7: Compare. At the end of the day, write down your total Screen Time and the minutes in the app you picked on Day 1. Compare both figures with Day 1. Then answer two questions: what was easiest, and what do you want to keep?

Reading your own result

Be honest with it. A drop between Day 1 and Day 7 might come from the plan, or from a calmer week, or from knowing you were measuring. In the study on limiting social media, both groups improved on some measures. Both groups monitored their use. Your seven days cannot separate those causes, and that is fine. What matters is whether you want to keep what you changed.

If you want a firmer answer, repeat the measurement a week later, without the changes, and compare.

What this does not do

This is not treatment for anything. If scrolling feels out of your control, or you feel low or anxious a lot of the time, talk to a doctor or a counsellor. This article gives no medical advice.

FAQ

Will seven days change my habit?

We cannot say. No study tested this sequence. It is designed to show you your own pattern and to try small changes.

What if I miss a day?

Skip to the next day. The point is to learn, not to keep a streak.

Do I need an app to do this?

No. Screen Time and a notebook are enough. Better Than Default can show you the steps for the settings, but the app cannot block anything or change a setting for you.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Satici, S. A., Gocet Tekin, E., Deniz, M. E., & Satici, B. (2023). Doomscrolling scale: Its association with personality traits, psychological distress, social media use, and wellbeing. Applied Research in Quality of Life, 18(2), 833-847. DOI: 10.1007/s11482-022-10110-7. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36275044/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: three studies (N = 378, 419, 460); doomscrolling associated with distress and, through distress, with lower well-being. What it does not show: cause and effect.
  2. Brown, L., & Kuss, D. J. (2020). Fear of missing out, mental wellbeing, and social connectedness: A seven-day social media abstinence trial. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 17(12), 4566. DOI: 10.3390/ijerph17124566. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32599962/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in 61 people, a week of abstinence went with higher well-being and connectedness and lower fear of missing out and phone use. What it does not show: a controlled comparison; there was no control group.
  3. Grüning, D. J., Riedel, F., & Lorenz-Spreen, P. (2023). Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 120(8), e2213114120. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2213114120. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36795756/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a six-week field experiment (N = 280) with a short pause before opening chosen apps. What it does not show: a control group in the field part (none described in the abstract). (Title left out because it contains the app's brand name.)
  4. Olson, J. A., Sandra, D. A., Chmoulevitch, D., Raz, A., & Veissiere, S. P. L. (2022). A nudge-based intervention to reduce problematic smartphone use: Randomised controlled trial. International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction, 1-23. DOI: 10.1007/s11469-022-00826-w. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35600564/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a ten-strategy package lowered problematic use and screen time and improved sleep quality versus a monitoring control (N = 70). What it does not show: which strategy mattered.
  5. Hunt, M. G., Marx, R., Lipson, C., & Young, J. (2018). No more FOMO: Limiting social media decreases loneliness and depression. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology. DOI: 10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751. Read: abstract (Semantic Scholar record; the publisher page refused automated access). Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: both groups improved on some measures over baseline, which the authors read as a benefit of self-monitoring. What it does not show: effects beyond three weeks. Volume and pages not checked, so not given.
