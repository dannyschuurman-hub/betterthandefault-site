Why a Gray Phone Feels Boring (and Whether That Is the Point)

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

The feeling

Switch your phone to grayscale and the first thing you notice is not a benefit. It is that everything looks flat. Photos lose their pull, icons stop shouting, and the screen feels like a tool again instead of a treat.

The popular explanation is that a duller screen is less rewarding, so you use it less. It is a neat story. This article checks how much of it the research supports, and we should say up front that the evidence is mixed in places and thin in others.

What the studies measured

Screen time. A pre-registered field experiment with 112 participants found that switching to grayscale cut objectively measured screen time compared with a group that only monitored its use, and it did so immediately. A one-week study with 84 participants, compared against each person's own baseline week, found daily screen time about 20 minutes lower, while the number of phone unlocks did not change. A third study with 240 participants completing the first questionnaire and 92 completing the second asked people to set their phones to grayscale for at least 24 hours. According to the abstract page we read, grayscale reduced usage time. The authors conclude that it is an effective way to cut use. Fewer than half of the people who completed the first questionnaire completed the second, and the page describes no control group.

A package of nudges. A randomised trial let participants choose among ten strategies, including grayscale and switching off non-essential notifications, and follow them for 2 to 6 weeks. In the second study, with 70 people, the group using the strategies had lower problematic smartphone use, lower screen time and better sleep quality than a control group that only monitored its screen time. Grayscale was one part of a bundle, so we cannot say how much of the effect came from the gray screen.

So far the picture is consistent: in four studies, grayscale or a bundle containing it went with less measured or reported phone time.

Where it gets mixed

Less screen time is not the same as feeling better. In the larger field experiment, the drop in screen time did not bring an immediate change in well-being or academic performance. In the one-week study, some self-reported feelings improved, such as sense of control, while productivity and sleep quality did not change. The two studies measured different things in different groups over different lengths of time, and each is small, so they do not contradict each other so much as leave the question open.

The honest summary: grayscale seems to lower the time you spend. Whether it improves how you feel is unclear.

Does boredom explain it?

This is the part nobody has shown. None of the studies we read measured boredom, or tested whether people used the phone less because it felt dull. It is a plausible idea and a tempting one, but it is a guess.

There is some related theory. One model of boredom describes it as a signal that your attention is not engaged in something that matters to you. If that is right, a gray screen might make scrolling feel less engaging, which would make a pointless check less rewarding. Equally, a dull screen could make you tap harder for stimulation. The model does not tell us which. It is a theory paper, not a test of grayscale.

There is also a practical caution from the one-week study: unlocks did not change. People picked the phone up just as often. So the effect seems to be in how long you stay on it, not in how often you reach for it. That fits a "less rewarding once you are there" story, but it does not prove it.

How to make it easier to stick with

  • Give it a week before judging. The first days feel strangest.
  • Know how to switch it back. Maps, charts and photo editing can be hard in gray. On iPhone: Settings, Accessibility, Display & Text Size, Color Filters. You can turn it off for a task and back on afterwards.
  • Do not switch it off every time something looks dull. The studies tested people who kept it on.

FAQ

Does grayscale reduce screen time?

In the studies we read, yes: measured screen time went down, in one study immediately compared with a control group, in another by about 20 minutes a day against a baseline week.

Does a gray screen make you feel better?

Mixed. One large experiment found no immediate well-being effect. Another study found some self-reported improvements. Neither is enough to promise anything.

Is a dull screen the reason it works?

Nobody has tested that. It is a reasonable idea, and it is still just an idea.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Zimmermann, L., & Sobolev, M. (2023). Digital strategies for screen time reduction: A randomized field experiment. Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking, 26(1), 42-49. DOI: 10.1089/cyber.2022.0027. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36577008/. Read: abstract (via PubMed record and Europe PMC). What it shows: grayscale cut objectively measured screen time versus monitoring only (N = 112). What it does not show: any immediate effect on well-being or academic performance.
  2. Dekker, C. A., & Baumgartner, S. (2023). Is life brighter when your phone is not? The efficacy of a grayscale smartphone intervention addressing digital well-being. Mobile Media & Communication. DOI: 10.1177/20501579231212062. Read: abstract (Semantic Scholar record; publisher page refused access). What it shows: with 84 participants, about 20 minutes less daily screen time, unlocks unchanged. What it does not show: productivity or sleep effects, or a separate control group.
  3. Wickord, L.-C., & Quaiser-Pohl, C. (2023). Suffering from problematic smartphone use? Why not use grayscale setting as an intervention! An experimental study. Computers in Human Behavior Reports, 10, 100294. DOI: 10.1016/j.chbr.2023.100294. Read: abstract (university repository page), summarised on opening; metadata via Crossref. Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: a repeated-measures experiment, 240 participants in the first questionnaire and 92 in the second, with reduced usage time after at least 24 hours of grayscale. What it does not show: effects beyond about a day, or a control group.
  4. Olson, J. A., Sandra, D. A., Chmoulevitch, D., Raz, A., & Veissiere, S. P. L. (2022). A nudge-based intervention to reduce problematic smartphone use: Randomised controlled trial. International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction, 1-23. DOI: 10.1007/s11469-022-00826-w. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35600564/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a bundle of ten strategies, including grayscale, lowered problematic use and screen time against a monitoring control (N = 70). What it does not show: which strategy did the work.
  5. Westgate, E. C., & Wilson, T. D. (2018). Boring thoughts and bored minds: The MAC model of boredom and cognitive engagement. Psychological Review, 125(5), 689-713. DOI: 10.1037/rev0000097. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29963873/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a theory of boredom as a sign of unsuccessful attentional engagement. What it does not show: anything about grayscale screens.
