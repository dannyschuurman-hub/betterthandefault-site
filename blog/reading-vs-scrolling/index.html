Reading vs. Scrolling: Two Kinds of Screen Time

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

Same screen, different activity

"Screen time" is one number on your phone, but it covers very different things. Reading an article to the end and flicking through a feed of short clips both use the same glass. They ask different things of your attention.

This article separates the two, and it is careful about what is actually known. There is good research on reading from paper versus screens. There is much less that compares reading with scrolling a feed. We will keep those apart.

What a large review says about paper and screens

A meta-analysis of studies from 2000 to 2017 compared reading comparable texts on paper and on digital devices. It included 38 between-participants studies and 16 within-participants studies, with 171,055 participants in total. Both designs showed a paper advantage for reading comprehension, with an effect size (g) of about minus 0.21, which is a small effect.

Three things changed the size of the effect. The paper advantage was larger when readers were under time pressure than when they read at their own pace. It held for informational texts and mixed texts but not for narrative-only texts. And it grew over the years covered by the studies.

Read that carefully. It does not say screens ruin reading. It says that in many studies, on average, people understood informational text a little better on paper, especially when rushed. For a story read at leisure, the review found no paper advantage. We read the abstract page of this paper, not the full text, so details such as how studies were grouped are not checked here.

What it does not tell us about feeds

The studies in that review are about reading texts. Scrolling a feed of short posts and videos is a different activity, and the review is not about it. We did not find a study that compares reading a long text with scrolling a feed on the same outcomes.

What we do have is nearby. In a survey-based set of three studies, a doomscrolling scale was associated with fear of missing out, problematic social media use and psychological distress. In a seven-day social media break with 61 people, interviews pointed to coping, habit and boredom as motives for scrolling. And in a pre-registered experiment with 308 employees, a short break spent on algorithmic video clips gave some recovery, but not full recovery, especially for fatigue, while nature did better.

None of this says that scrolling is bad in itself. It says that scrolling is often a habit driven by boredom and unease, and that as a break it did less in the one study that tested it.

Why boredom comes into it

In 11 studies, participants typically did not enjoy spending 6 to 15 minutes alone in a room with nothing to do but think, and many preferred doing something external, even something unpleasant. A model of boredom describes it as a signal that attention is not engaged in something valued. A feed removes that signal very quickly. A long text asks you to stay with it until it becomes interesting.

That is a reason to expect that reading feels harder than scrolling at first, which may be why people choose the feed. It is not a finding that reading is better for you.

Small ways to make room for reading

These are our suggestions, not tested methods.

  1. Put one book or long article where the feed icon was. Move short-video apps off the first screen and put a reading app or a book on it.
  2. Give reading a time that already exists. Ten minutes after dinner, or in a queue, beats a plan for an hour on Sunday.
  3. Read narrative first if you are rusty. The paper advantage in the review did not show for narrative-only texts, and a story is easier to stay with.
  4. For dense material, consider paper. If you are under time pressure and need to understand an informational text, the review gives a small reason to print it.
  5. Count pages, not minutes. A page is a finish line your feed does not have.

FAQ

Is reading on paper better than reading on a screen?

For informational texts, a large meta-analysis found a small paper advantage in comprehension, larger under time pressure. For narrative-only texts it found none.

Is scrolling worse than reading?

No study we read compares them directly. Scrolling as a break gave only partial recovery in one study, and scrolling is often driven by habit.

Should I stop scrolling?

That is up to you. This article gives no medical advice, and nothing here promises a result.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Delgado, P., Vargas, C., Ackerman, R., & Salmerón, L. (2018). Don't throw away your printed books: A meta-analysis on the effects of reading media on reading comprehension. Educational Research Review, 25, 23-38. DOI: 10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003. Read: abstract page (university repository), summarised on opening. Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: 38 between-participants and 16 within-participants studies, 171,055 participants, a small paper advantage (g = -0.21) larger under time pressure and for informational texts. What it does not show: results for feeds or short-form content; full text not read.
  2. Satici, S. A., Gocet Tekin, E., Deniz, M. E., & Satici, B. (2023). Doomscrolling scale: Its association with personality traits, psychological distress, social media use, and wellbeing. Applied Research in Quality of Life, 18(2), 833-847. DOI: 10.1007/s11482-022-10110-7. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36275044/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: three studies (N = 378, 419, 460); doomscrolling associated with distress and, through distress, with lower well-being. What it does not show: cause and effect.
  3. Brown, L., & Kuss, D. J. (2020). Fear of missing out, mental wellbeing, and social connectedness: A seven-day social media abstinence trial. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 17(12), 4566. DOI: 10.3390/ijerph17124566. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32599962/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: interviews pointing to coping, habit and boredom as motives for scrolling (N = 61). What it does not show: a controlled comparison.
  4. Grobelny, J., Glinka, M., & Chirkowska-Smolak, T. (2024). The impact of hedonic social media use during microbreaks on employee resources recovery. Scientific Reports, 14, 21603. DOI: 10.1038/s41598-024-72825-x. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39284901/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in a pre-registered experiment with 308 employees, video microbreaks gave partial recovery, nature did better. What it does not show: long-term effects.
  5. Wilson, T. D., Reinhard, D. A., Westgate, E. C., Gilbert, D. T., Ellerbeck, N., Hahn, C., Brown, C. L., & Shaked, A. (2014). Social psychology: Just think: The challenges of the disengaged mind. Science, 345(6192), 75-77. DOI: 10.1126/science.1250830. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24994650/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in 11 studies, people typically did not enjoy 6 to 15 minutes alone with their thoughts. What it does not show: anything about feeds or reading.
  6. Westgate, E. C., & Wilson, T. D. (2018). Boring thoughts and bored minds: The MAC model of boredom and cognitive engagement. Psychological Review, 125(5), 689-713. DOI: 10.1037/rev0000097. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29963873/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a theory of boredom as unsuccessful attentional engagement. What it does not show: a test with feeds.
