Does Grayscale on Your Phone Help? What the Research Says

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

The idea in one minute

Your phone is full of colour. Red badges, bright icons, vivid photos. The idea behind grayscale is simple: if the screen looks less rewarding, you may pick it up less, or put it down sooner. Every iPhone can do this with a built-in colour filter, so it costs nothing and takes about a minute.

It is a popular tip, so it is fair to ask whether it holds up. Two studies are worth knowing about. Both are short, both are small, and both tell a slightly different story depending on what you were hoping for.

What the studies found

The first is a pre-registered field experiment with 112 participants over several weeks. People were split into three groups: one switched their phone to grayscale, one set a self-chosen time limit, and one only monitored their own use. The grayscale group showed an immediate and significant drop in objectively measured screen time compared with the monitoring group. The time-limit group also reduced screen time, but by less, and more gradually.

The second is a one-week study with 84 participants. Their phone use was tracked for two weeks: the first week as a baseline, the second week with grayscale on. Daily screen time fell by about 20 minutes. The number of times people unlocked their phone did not change, which the authors read as a sign of deep-rooted checking habits. People also reported feeling more in control of their phone use, and less overuse, less online vigilance and less stress.

Put together: in both studies, grayscale went along with less measured time on the phone. That is the part with the most support.

What they did not show

Here the honest part matters, because "less screen time" is not the same as "a better life".

In the larger experiment, the drop in screen time did not lead to an immediate change in subjective well-being or in academic performance. The authors point out that this goes against the popular belief that cutting screen time brings broad benefits. In the smaller study, productivity and sleep quality did not change either, even though some self-reported feelings improved.

There are more limits. The abstracts we read do not describe who took part in detail, so we cannot say how well the results carry over to you. The smaller study compared each person with their own baseline week and, as far as its abstract describes, had no separate control group, so other things that changed that week could play a role. Both studies are short. Nobody knows what happens after six months, when the novelty has worn off and the screen looks normal again.

So a fair summary is this: grayscale is a cheap way to use your phone a bit less. It is not a proven way to feel better.

How to switch it on

On an iPhone: Settings, then Accessibility, then Display & Text Size, then Color Filters. Turn on Color Filters and choose Grayscale. To undo it, follow the same path and switch it off. Some people find that photos, maps and anything with colour-coded information become harder to use, so it is worth knowing how to turn it back on quickly when you need colour.

A small practical note: the effect probably depends on you actually leaving it on. If you switch it off every time something looks dull, you are back to the default.

FAQ

Does grayscale make you use your phone less?

In the two studies above, yes, measured screen time went down: in one study immediately and significantly compared with a control group, in the other by about 20 minutes a day compared with the participants' own baseline week. Unlock counts did not change in the smaller study.

Does a gray screen improve well-being or sleep?

The evidence is mixed. The larger experiment found no immediate effect on well-being or academic performance. The smaller study reported some improvements in self-reported control and stress, but no change in productivity or sleep quality. Neither study is enough to promise a benefit.

Is it safe to leave grayscale on all the time?

It is a display filter you can switch off at any moment. It may make some tasks harder, for example reading maps or colour-coded charts. This article does not give medical advice; if a display setting causes you trouble, turn it off.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Zimmermann, L., & Sobolev, M. (2023). Digital strategies for screen time reduction: A randomized field experiment. Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking, 26(1), 42-49. DOI: 10.1089/cyber.2022.0027. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36577008/. Read: abstract (via PubMed record and Europe PMC). What it shows: in a pre-registered field experiment with 112 participants, grayscale cut objectively measured screen time versus a monitoring-only group, and time-limit goals had a smaller, slower effect. What it does not show: any immediate effect on well-being or academic performance; the abstract gives no effect sizes or detail on who took part.
  2. Dekker, C. A., & Baumgartner, S. (2023). Is life brighter when your phone is not? The efficacy of a grayscale smartphone intervention addressing digital well-being. Mobile Media & Communication. DOI: doi.org/10.1177/20501579231212062. Read: abstract (via the Semantic Scholar record; the publisher page refused automated access). What it shows: with 84 participants tracked for one baseline week and one grayscale week, daily screen time fell by about 20 minutes while unlocks did not change, and self-reported control improved. What it does not show: effects on productivity or sleep quality (none found), or what happens beyond one week; no separate control group is described in the abstract.
