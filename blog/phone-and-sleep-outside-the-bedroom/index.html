Phone and Sleep: Charging Outside the Bedroom and Putting It Away Before Bed

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

Two ideas, two different levels of evidence

Two habits come up again and again when people talk about phones and sleep. One is to stop using the phone for a while before bed. The other is to charge it outside the bedroom, so it is not within reach when you wake in the night or lie there not sleeping.

They sound alike, but the evidence behind them is not the same. One has a small randomised trial. The other, as far as we can tell, has not been tested on its own. We will keep them apart.

This is general information about habits, not medical advice. If you have trouble sleeping that worries you, a doctor is the right person to ask.

The 30 minutes before bed

A randomised pilot trial gave 38 participants either an instruction to avoid their mobile phone for 30 minutes before bedtime, or no instruction at all. According to the paper, they were university students with poor sleep who used their phone in bed for more than 30 minutes. The abstract reports that restricting phone use before bedtime for four weeks went with less time to fall asleep, more time asleep, better sleep quality, less pre-sleep arousal, more positive mood and better working memory. (The Methods section of the full text refers to a one-week trial, so the exact length is not consistent within the paper.)

That sounds encouraging, and it is, within limits. The trial was small, with 19 people in each group. Sleep was measured with a diary and a questionnaire rather than with objective devices, and the authors say that may not be very objective. All participants were motivated to cut back, so the result may not apply to people who are not. This is a pilot, which means it is a first step, not a conclusion.

Phone use at bedtime and sleep in adults

A survey of 844 adults in Flanders aged 18 to 94 asked about electronic media use and sleep habits. Messaging or calling after lights out predicted worse scores on a sleep quality questionnaire, including longer time to fall asleep, worse sleep efficiency, more sleep disturbance and more daytime dysfunction. Bedtime phone use also predicted a later self-reported rise time and more fatigue. Age changed the pattern: in younger respondents it went with more fatigue and later rise times, while in older respondents it went with earlier rise times and shorter sleep.

The limits matter. A cross-sectional survey measures things at one moment. It can show that bedtime phone use and worse sleep go together. It cannot show which comes first: it may be that people who sleep badly pick up their phones, not the other way around. It is also from the mid-2010s, when only half of the respondents owned a smartphone.

The phone in the bedroom, in young people

A systematic review and meta-analysis included 20 cross-sectional studies covering 125,198 children and teenagers (mean age 14.5 years). Using a media device at bedtime was associated with inadequate sleep quantity (odds ratio 2.17), poor sleep quality (1.46) and excessive daytime sleepiness (2.72). Simply having access to a device at night, without using it, was also associated with worse outcomes (odds ratios 1.79, 1.53 and 2.27). The studies differed a lot from one another (the review reports heterogeneity of 24 to 90 percent), and the authors call for more research on how these devices influence sleep.

This is the closest thing to evidence for "keep it outside the bedroom", and it is not very close. It is about children and teenagers, not adults. All 20 studies were cross-sectional, so the review cannot show that removing the phone would improve anyone's sleep. And "access" in those studies is not the same as charging it in the hall.

What we can say, and what we cannot

We can say that bedtime phone use and worse sleep go together in surveys of adults and young people, and that one small trial found that not using the phone in the last 30 minutes went with better sleep measures.

We cannot say that charging the phone in another room will improve your sleep. Nobody we read tested that. It is a sensible idea, and it is easy to try, but it is advice.

Two ways to try it

  1. Wind down without the phone. Set a time 30 minutes before bed. Put the phone away from the bed and do something that does not involve a screen: read a paper book, stretch or tidy up.
  2. Charge it elsewhere. Pick a spot outside the bedroom for the charger, and buy or use a simple alarm clock for the bedside table, so your alarm is not a reason to keep the phone near you.

FAQ

Is it better to charge my phone outside the bedroom?

It may be, but it has not been tested on its own. The studies we read link bedtime use, and in young people also night-time access, to worse sleep in surveys, which cannot prove cause. It is our advice, and cheap to try.

How long before bed should I stop using my phone?

One small trial used 30 minutes. That does not make 30 the right number for everyone. It is a promising start, nothing more.

Will this fix my sleep problems?

No one can promise that. If you have persistent trouble sleeping, please talk to a doctor.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. He, J.-W., Tu, Z.-H., Xiao, L., Su, T., & Tang, Y.-X. (2020). Effect of restricting bedtime mobile phone use on sleep, arousal, mood, and working memory: A randomized pilot trial. PLoS ONE, 15(2), e0228756. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0228756. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32040492/. Read: full text (journals.plos.org, summarised on opening) and abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in 38 randomised participants, a 30-minute pre-bed restriction went with better sleep measures, arousal, mood and working memory. What it does not show: results in a larger or less motivated group; the sleep measures were diary and questionnaire, and the duration is inconsistent within the paper (four weeks in the abstract, one week in Methods).
  2. Exelmans, L., & Van den Bulck, J. (2016). Bedtime mobile phone use and sleep in adults. Social Science & Medicine. DOI: 10.1016/j.socscimed.2015.11.037. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26688552/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in 844 Flemish adults, bedtime phone use predicted worse self-reported sleep outcomes, with differences by age. What it does not show: cause and effect, because it is a cross-sectional survey from a time of lower smartphone ownership.
  3. Carter, B., Rees, P., Hale, L., Bhattacharjee, D., & Paradkar, M. S. (2016). Association between portable screen-based media device access or use and sleep outcomes: A systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA Pediatrics. DOI: 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27802500/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: across the 20 cross-sectional studies it included (125,198 children and teenagers, mean age 14.5), device use or access at bedtime was associated with poorer sleep outcomes. What it does not show: that removing devices improves sleep, or anything about adults; heterogeneity between studies was high.
