Deep Work for Beginners: Start Smaller Than You Think

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

What people mean by deep work

"Deep work" is a casual name for something simple: time spent on one demanding task, without interruptions, where you can think properly. Writing a report, learning a hard topic, solving a problem that needs several steps held in your head at once.

It is not a scientific term with a single definition, and no study we read tests "deep work" as such. What we can look at is the research on the parts it is made of: what switching between tasks costs, what interruptions do, and what breaks do. Put together, that gives a reasonable way to start. It does not give a guarantee.

What switching between tasks costs

Researchers have measured the cost of switching for decades. In four experiments in which people alternated between tasks or repeated the same task, alternating produced switching-time costs. Those costs grew when the rules were more complex and shrank when there was a cue about which task to do. The tasks were lab tasks such as classifying shapes and solving arithmetic, so this is evidence about the mechanism, not about your inbox.

A second line of work looks at what stays behind. In two experiments on what is called attention residue, an unfinished task seemed to hold on to attention, and performance on the next task was lower. A later set of four studies found that a short "ready-to-resume" plan, where you briefly note how you will come back to the interrupted task, reduced the effect.

That last finding is the most useful one for beginners. It suggests that how you stop matters, not only how you start.

What heavy task juggling goes with

One widely cited study compared people who often use several media at once with people who do so rarely. The heavy group was more susceptible to interference from irrelevant stimuli and, surprisingly, did worse on a task-switching test. A later review is more careful: the balance of evidence suggests heavier media multitaskers do worse in several cognitive areas, but many studies find no difference, and the review says the direction of cause is not settled. So heavy multitasking goes with worse performance on some tests. It does not show that multitasking made people worse.

Start with a block you can finish

Nothing in this research tells you how long a first deep-work block should be, so the advice below is ours.

  1. Pick one task, and write it as a single sentence. "Draft the introduction" is a task. "Work on the report" is a topic.
  2. Start with 25 to 30 minutes. In a one-day study with 87 students, people who took planned breaks reported less fatigue and distractedness and more concentration than people who chose their own break timing, with similar task completion. That study used blocks of 12 and 24 minutes, which fits the idea of starting small, but it says nothing about the best length. It is one day and one group, so hold it loosely.
  3. Remove one obvious interruption first. Close the tab, flip the phone over or put it in another room. Choose one thing, not ten.
  4. End with a note. In the last minute write one line: where you stopped and what comes next. This is our adaptation of the ready-to-resume idea.
  5. Take a real break. Stand, look outside, drink some water. A meta-analysis of 22 samples (N = 2,335) found small effects of micro-breaks on vigor (d = .36) and fatigue (d = .35), and a non-significant effect on overall performance (d = .16).
  6. Add five minutes when it feels easy. Grow the block slowly over weeks. There is no tested target.

If your attention wanders

If two hours feels daunting, start with a shorter block. A short block that you finish is easier to repeat than a long one you abandon. If you notice your mind wandering, that is normal; write the stray thought down and come back.

FAQ

How long should a deep work session be?

The studies we discuss here give no clear answer. Try a block you can finish, such as 25 to 30 minutes, and make it longer if that feels manageable.

Do I need to turn the phone off?

Out of sight is a sensible first step. Studies on where your phone sits do exist, but we did not review them, so we call this advice, not a result.

Is multitasking really that bad?

Switching has measurable costs in lab tasks. Whether heavy multitaskers are worse because of multitasking is not settled.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Rubinstein, J. S., Meyer, D. E., & Evans, J. E. (2001). Executive control of cognitive processes in task switching. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 27(4), 763-797. DOI: 10.1037/0096-1523.27.4.763. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11518143/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in four lab experiments, switching tasks took extra time, more with complex rules and less with cues. What it does not show: sample sizes (not in the abstract), or effects in office work.
  2. Leroy, S. (2009). Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 109(2), 168-181. DOI: 10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002. Read: abstract (RePEc page), summarised on opening. Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: two experiments in which an unfinished task left attention behind. What it does not show: sample size (not in the abstract).
  3. Leroy, S., & Glomb, T. M. (2018). Tasks interrupted: How anticipating time pressure on resumption of an interrupted task causes attention residue and low performance on interrupting tasks and how a "ready-to-resume" plan mitigates the effects. Organization Science, 29, 380-397. DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2017.1184. Read: abstract (Semantic Scholar record). Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: across four studies, a ready-to-resume plan reduced attention residue. What it does not show: sample sizes (not in the abstract).
  4. Ophir, E., Nass, C., & Wagner, A. D. (2009). Cognitive control in media multitaskers. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 106(37), 15583-15587. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.0903620106. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19706386/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: heavy media multitaskers were more susceptible to interference and worse at a task-switching test. What it does not show: cause and effect, or sample sizes (not in the abstract).
  5. Uncapher, M. R., & Wagner, A. D. (2018). Minds and brains of media multitaskers: Current findings and future directions. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 115(40), 9889-9896. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1611612115. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30275312/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a review in which heavier media multitaskers often, but not always, performed worse. What it does not show: whether media multitasking causes the differences.
  6. Biwer, F., Wiradhany, W., Oude Egbrink, M. G. A., & de Bruin, A. B. H. (2023). British Journal of Educational Psychology, 93(Suppl 2), 353-367. DOI: 10.1111/bjep.12593. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in one day with 87 students, planned breaks went with better mood and similar task completion in less time than self-regulated breaks. What it does not show: effects beyond one day or for other groups. (Title left out because it contains a technique's brand name.)

A systematic review and meta-analysis on the efficacy of micro-breaks for increasing well-being and performance. PLoS ONE, 17(8), e0272460. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0272460. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36044424/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: 22 samples, N = 2,335; small effects on vigor and fatigue, non-significant effect on performance.
