How to Set a VIP List: Let the Right People Through, Silence the Rest

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

Why a list, and not a switch

Silencing everything is simple. It is also uncomfortable when someone might need you. In a small study of people who turned off notifications for a day, some felt anxious because they could not respond as quickly as others expected, and some felt less connected to their social groups. They also felt less distracted and more productive. Calm and worry arrived together.

A VIP list is an attempt to keep the first part and soften the second. While Focus is on, you can allow notifications and calls from the people you choose. Other alerts depend on your Focus settings. It is a simple idea, and it is worth saying right away that no study we read tested a VIP list itself. It is our advice, built on the research around it.

What the surrounding research tells us

Notifications cost attention. In a lab experiment, a notification alone disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task, even when people did not touch the phone. That is a lab task, so it tells us why people look for relief, not how much a ping costs you.

Silence has a price. In the one-day study above, which we read as a preprint, 30 volunteers turned off notifications on all devices. Participants felt less distracted and more productive, some felt anxious about slow replies, and about half said two years later that they were still following through with their plans. The small sample and the one-day length matter here.

Batching and silence were each compared with usual notifications in one trial. In a randomised field experiment with 237 people, notifications delivered three times a day went with feeling more attentive, in a better mood and more in control, while no notifications went with more anxiety and fear of missing out. Nobody in that trial sorted notifications by sender, so this step is ours.

Disabling the non-essential is a common part of nudge packages. In a randomised trial of ten strategies, participants could pick among them, including disabling non-essential notifications and switching to grayscale. The group using the strategies had lower problematic smartphone use and screen time than a monitoring-only control group. We cannot say how much of this came from notifications.

None of this tests "keep a few people, silence the rest". What it gives is a reasonable logic: if silence brings worry, and clutter brings distraction, a short list is a middle road.

How to set one up on an iPhone

Menus change between iOS versions, so use these steps as a guide.

  1. Open Settings, then Focus, and choose Do Not Disturb or another focus mode you like.
  2. Under People, choose Allow Notifications From, and add the few people you want. Think of a partner, a parent, a child's school, a manager if your job needs it.
  3. Under Apps, decide which apps may still notify you. For many people the answer is none.
  4. Check the calls setting. You can allow calls from the same list, so a real emergency can still ring.
  5. Schedule it, or switch it on yourself when you start a block of work.

Keep the list short

Start with the people whose calls you would interrupt your work to answer, perhaps three to five. A list of 20 people is the phone all over again. If you add someone every week, ask whether they need to reach you instantly or within the day. Many things can wait for the next batch.

Tell the people on the list what it means. A short message, "I silence my phone while I work, but I have set it so that your calls can still get through," costs nothing and may take some of the worry out of being quiet. That is our suggestion, not a tested step.

What a VIP list does not do

It does not stop you from checking your phone. It does not silence the pull of a feed you open yourself. And it does not solve the problem of a boss who expects instant answers. For that you may need a conversation, not a setting.

FAQ

Is there evidence that a VIP list works?

Not directly. The research around it shows that notifications cost attention and that silencing everything can bring worry. A list is our attempt to sit between the two.

Who belongs on the list?

Only people who may need to reach you urgently. Start small and adjust after a week.

Can the app set the list for me?

No. Apple does not allow apps to change these settings. Better Than Default shows the steps and opens the right screen where Apple allows it.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. Pielot, M., & Rello, L. (2017). Productive, anxious, lonely: 24 hours without push notifications. arXiv preprint, version 3 (first posted 2016). DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.1612.02314 (the conference version has DOI 10.1145/3098279.3098526; not opened). Read: abstract page on arXiv, summarised on opening. What it shows: with notifications off for 24 hours, 30 volunteers felt less distracted and more productive, some felt anxious about slow replies and less connected, and about half were still following through two years later. What it does not show: effects beyond a day, or anything about sender lists.
  2. Fitz, N., Kushlev, K., Jagannathan, R., Lewis, T., Paliwal, D., & Ariely, D. (2019). Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being. Computers in Human Behavior, 101, 84-94. DOI: 10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016. Read: full text (open PDF copy). Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: batching three times a day went with feeling more attentive, in a better mood and more in control; no notifications went with more anxiety. What it does not show: sorting by sender, or effects beyond two weeks.
  3. Stothart, C., Mitchum, A., & Yehnert, C. (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 41(4), 893-897. DOI: 10.1037/xhp0000100. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121498/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: notifications alone disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task. What it does not show: sample size (not in the abstract), or real-life effects.
  4. Olson, J. A., Sandra, D. A., Chmoulevitch, D., Raz, A., & Veissiere, S. P. L. (2022). A nudge-based intervention to reduce problematic smartphone use: Randomised controlled trial. International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction, 1-23. DOI: 10.1007/s11469-022-00826-w. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35600564/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a ten-strategy package, including disabling non-essential notifications, lowered problematic use and screen time versus a monitoring control (N = 70). What it does not show: which strategy mattered.
