An Evening Phone Routine You Can Try

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

Why the evening is the interesting part

The evening is when many phones get the most unplanned time. The day's work is over, the sofa is comfortable, and a feed asks for nothing. It is also the stretch right before sleep, which is why so much research looks at it.

This article does not promise better sleep. It looks at what the studies found, how strong each one is, and then offers a routine built from small steps. You can change any of it.

What the research found

A small trial of restricting the phone before bed. In a randomised pilot trial with 38 participants, one group was told to avoid their mobile phone for 30 minutes before bedtime and another group got no instruction. According to the paper, they were university students with poor sleep who used their phone in bed for more than 30 minutes. The abstract reports that the restricted group fell asleep faster, slept longer and reported better sleep quality, less pre-sleep arousal, more positive mood and better working memory. The abstract says four weeks, while the Methods section of the full text refers to a one-week trial, so the exact length is unclear. It is a pilot with 38 people, and the sleep measures were diaries and questionnaires.

Adults who use the phone after lights out. A cross-sectional survey of 844 Flemish adults found that sending or receiving messages or calls after lights out predicted worse sleep scores, including a longer time to fall asleep and more daytime dysfunction. The relationship varied by age. A survey can show that two things go together, not which causes which.

A lab comparison of light-emitting screens. In a laboratory study, compared with when they read a printed book, participants took longer to fall asleep after reading on a light-emitting device. They also felt less sleepy in the evening and less alert the next morning, their melatonin secretion was lower and their body clocks shifted later. This study used an e-reader, not a phone, and the abstract does not give the number of participants.

Young people with devices in the bedroom. A meta-analysis of 20 cross-sectional studies with 125,198 children and teenagers (mean age 14.5) found that bedtime use of a media device was associated with inadequate sleep quantity (odds ratio 2.17), as well as poor sleep quality and excessive daytime sleepiness. It is not about adults, and it is again association.

A package of nudges. In a randomised trial of ten strategies, including turning notifications off and switching to grayscale, the group that followed the strategies reported better sleep quality than a monitoring-only control group. Sleep was one outcome of a bundle, so we cannot link it to any one step.

What this adds up to

The pieces fit together in a loose way: phone use near bedtime goes with worse sleep measures in surveys, one small trial of leaving the phone alone for 30 minutes went with better sleep measures, and a lab study points at light-emitting screens as one possible route. None of it proves that your phone is why you sleep badly, and none of it is medical advice. If you have trouble sleeping, a doctor is the right person to ask.

A routine to try

Keep it short enough that you will actually do it.

  1. Pick a stop time for the phone. Thirty minutes before bed is the length the small trial used. Start with 15 if that is what you can keep.
  2. Charge it away from the bed. This part is our advice. It makes the stop easier because the phone is not within reach.
  3. Set the next day's one task. Write it on paper or a note app before you stop. It gives the evening an end.
  4. Replace the phone with something small. A book, a stretch, a glass of water, tidying one surface. Reading a printed book was the comparison in the lab study.
  5. Use a real alarm. A simple alarm clock lets you charge your phone away from the bed.
  6. Judge after a week. Did falling asleep feel different? There is no number to hit.

FAQ

Does a phone-free half hour improve sleep?

In one small pilot trial of 38 people, it went with better sleep measures. It is one study, and the duration is inconsistent within the paper.

Is blue light the problem?

The lab study found effects of reading on a light-emitting e-reader compared with a printed book. We did not find a study that tests a phone's evening mode against not using the phone, so we cannot say.

What if I need my phone for an alarm?

Charge it across the room and use only the alarm. That keeps the habit change small.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. He, J.-W., Tu, Z.-H., Xiao, L., Su, T., & Tang, Y.-X. (2020). Effect of restricting bedtime mobile phone use on sleep, arousal, mood, and working memory: A randomized pilot trial. PLoS ONE, 15(2), e0228756. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0228756. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32040492/. Read: full text (journals.plos.org, summarised on opening) and abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in 38 randomised participants, a 30-minute pre-bed restriction went with better sleep measures, arousal, mood and working memory. What it does not show: results in a larger or less motivated group; the duration is inconsistent within the paper (four weeks in the abstract, one week in Methods).
  2. Exelmans, L., & Van den Bulck, J. (2016). Bedtime mobile phone use and sleep in adults. Social Science & Medicine, 148, 93-101. DOI: 10.1016/j.socscimed.2015.11.037. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26688552/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in 844 adults, bedtime phone use went with worse sleep scores. What it does not show: cause and effect.
  3. Chang, A.-M., Aeschbach, D., Duffy, J. F., & Czeisler, C. A. (2015). Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 112(4), 1232-1237. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1418490112. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25535358/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: reading on a light-emitting e-book device delayed sleep onset and the circadian clock compared with a printed book. What it does not show: sample size (not in the abstract), or effects of a phone.
  4. Carter, B., Rees, P., Hale, L., Bhattacharjee, D., & Paradkar, M. S. (2016). Association between portable screen-based media device access or use and sleep outcomes: A systematic review and meta-analysis. JAMA Pediatrics, 170(12), 1202-1208. DOI: 10.1001/jamapediatrics.2016.2341. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27802500/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in 20 cross-sectional studies (125,198 children and teenagers, mean age 14.5), bedtime device use went with inadequate sleep quantity (odds ratio 2.17). What it does not show: results for adults, or cause and effect.
  5. Olson, J. A., Sandra, D. A., Chmoulevitch, D., Raz, A., & Veissiere, S. P. L. (2022). A nudge-based intervention to reduce problematic smartphone use: Randomised controlled trial. International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction, 1-23. DOI: 10.1007/s11469-022-00826-w. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35600564/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a ten-strategy package improved sleep quality versus a monitoring control (N = 70). What it does not show: which strategy mattered.
