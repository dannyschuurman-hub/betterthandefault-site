What "Flow State" Actually Means (and What a Timer Cannot Do)

By Better Than Default. Sources checked 23 November 2026. About 4 minutes to read. Not medical advice.

A word that gets used for everything

"Flow" has become a catch-all for any good, focused work session. Say you were in flow, and people nod. But the word started as a description of a particular experience, and researchers have spent years trying to pin down what that experience is, and what goes on in the body when it happens.

This article puts it in our own words and sticks to what the studies we read can support. The short version: flow is a real, describable state that people report. It is not something a timer, a playlist or an app can promise to give you.

What researchers mean by flow

Put plainly, flow is the experience of being completely absorbed in what you are doing. Attention feels steady and almost effortless. You are less aware of yourself. The task itself is enjoyable. Researchers have described it as a subjective experience of effortless attention, reduced self-awareness and enjoyment that typically shows up during optimal performance of a task. A review of studies on flow in video games phrases the core of it as intense involvement with high concentration and focused attention, together with a sense of pleasure.

Notice that every part of that definition is about how it feels from the inside. That is why researchers measure it mostly by asking people to rate their experience, and then try to link those ratings to what can be measured in the body.

What the studies measured

Pianists playing the same piece five times. In a study of professional classical pianists, each musician played a piece five times and rated their flow after each. Higher flow ratings went with differences in heart period, blood pressure, heart rate variability, activity of a facial muscle linked to smiling, and the depth of breathing. The authors propose that flow is a state of effortless attention that arises when positive mood and high attention come together. The abstract does not give the number of pianists.

A puzzle game at three difficulty levels. In a study with 77 participants, people played a falling-block puzzle game at three difficulty levels: easy, optimal and difficult. The optimal level produced the highest levels of flow, positive mood and effortless attention. Higher flow went with deeper breathing, which the authors read as a more relaxed state. The study found no link between flow and the oxygen levels it measured in the frontal cortex.

A review of the physical and brain correlates. A comprehensive review of flow in video-game research noted that the results are inconsistent when it comes to a single underlying mechanism. Even so, it found associations between reported markers and several aspects of the experience: arousal, attention control, reward processing, automaticity and self-referential processing.

Flow and well-being. A meta-analysis found a moderately strong, positive relationship between flow and well-being. The relationship was stronger for some kinds of well-being than for others and was robust across many features of the studies. The abstract describes an association, and does not say that flow causes well-being.

What this does and does not tell you

In the puzzle-game study, the optimal difficulty level, between easy and difficult, produced the most flow and the most positive mood. Higher flow ratings also went with deeper breathing, which the authors read as a more relaxed state. That is useful.

But there are clear limits. Much of this work is on skilled people or on games, in a lab. Most measures depend on self-ratings. The physical findings are mixed, as the review says. And none of it shows that you can switch flow on at will. The one thing the puzzle-game study suggests is that the match between the challenge and the person matters. Easy and difficult versions did not produce as much flow in that study.

So "how to get into flow" articles that promise a method are promising more than the research says. At best, you can set up conditions in which flow becomes more likely.

Setting up the conditions, without any promises

  1. One task, clearly defined. You cannot be absorbed in three things.
  2. A level that is not boring and not overwhelming. If it feels too easy, add a constraint. If it feels too hard, cut it into a smaller piece.
  3. No interruptions for a set time. Ideally the phone is out of sight.
  4. A clear stopping point. Knowing when it ends makes it easier to start.

Even then, some sessions will feel effortless and some will not. That is normal.

FAQ

Is flow the same as just being focused?

Not quite. Focus can feel hard and effortful. In the studies above, flow is described as focused attention that feels effortless and enjoyable, with less self-awareness.

Can a timer or an app put me in flow?

No. Nothing we read shows that a timer produces flow. A timer can mark out a block of time. What happens inside the block depends on the task and on you.

Is flow good for me?

A meta-analysis found a positive association between flow and well-being. It describes a relationship, not a cause, and this is not medical advice.

What the research says

Only studies we opened and read are listed. Each entry says what we read and what the study does not show.

  1. de Manzano, Ö., Theorell, T., Harmat, L., & Ullén, F. (2010). The psychophysiology of flow during piano playing. Emotion. DOI: 10.1037/a0018432. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20515220/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in professional pianists playing a piece five times, flow ratings went with heart period, blood pressure, heart rate variability, a facial muscle and breathing depth. What it does not show: sample size (not in the abstract), or that results hold for non-experts.
  2. Harmat, L., de Manzano, Ö., Theorell, T., Högman, L., Fischer, H., & Ullén, F. (2015). Physiological correlates of the flow experience during computer game playing. International Journal of Psychophysiology. DOI: 10.1016/j.ijpsycho.2015.05.001. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25956190/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in 77 participants, the optimal difficulty level gave the highest flow, positive affect and effortless attention; higher flow went with deeper breathing; no link with frontal oxygenation. What it does not show: results for tasks other than a game, or any way of causing flow.
  3. Khoshnoud, S., Alvarez Igarzábal, F., & Wittmann, M. (2020). Peripheral-physiological and neural correlates of the flow experience while playing video games: A comprehensive review. PeerJ. DOI: 10.7717/peerj.10520. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33384898/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: an inconsistent picture across studies, with some associations with arousal, attention control, reward processing, automaticity and self-referential processing. What it does not show: one unified mechanism.
  4. Sweeny, K., Medina Huerta, J., Hawes, J., & Susoeff, S. (2026). Just keep flowing: A meta-analysis on the relationship between flow and well-being. Emotion. DOI: 10.1037/emo0001657. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41817600/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a moderately strong, positive relationship between flow and well-being across studies. What it does not show: cause and effect; the abstract gives no number of studies or participants.

How Better Than Default applies this

Better Than Default has a focus sprint timer: 30, 60 or 90 minutes, one task on screen, optional brown noise, then a real break. It does not create flow, and it does not measure it. A flow feature is not part of the current app.

What the sprint does is simple: it sets up a block with one task and no planned interruptions. If you drift into flow, good. If not, you still did a block of focused work. That framing is our own advice and nothing more: Our advice, not proven.

If you want to see how a sprint feels, you could start with one 30-minute block and notice whether the time passes differently. Either answer is fine.
