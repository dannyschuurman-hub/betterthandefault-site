The Doomscrolling Reset in 7 Days: A Trial You Run on Yourself

Dit artikel is in het Engels. Hieronder staat een korte Nederlandse samenvatting. Het is geen vertaling van het hele artikel en geen medisch advies.

Samenvatting

Dit is een proef die je zelf op jezelf doet: zeven dagen, elke dag één kleine verandering, met je eigen Schermtijd-cijfers ervoor en erna. Er zijn geen resultaten van dit plan en geen studie testte precies deze reeks; het is opgebouwd uit onderzoek naar verwante stappen. Het krijgt het label "Our advice, not proven" en is geen medisch advies.

Bronnen

De bronnen staan in het Engels, zoals in het artikel. We vermelden alleen studies die we zelf hebben geopend en gelezen.

  1. Satici, S. A., Gocet Tekin, E., Deniz, M. E., & Satici, B. (2023). Doomscrolling scale: Its association with personality traits, psychological distress, social media use, and wellbeing. Applied Research in Quality of Life, 18(2), 833-847. DOI: 10.1007/s11482-022-10110-7. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36275044/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: three studies (N = 378, 419, 460); doomscrolling associated with distress and, through distress, with lower well-being. What it does not show: cause and effect.
  2. Brown, L., & Kuss, D. J. (2020). Fear of missing out, mental wellbeing, and social connectedness: A seven-day social media abstinence trial. International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 17(12), 4566. DOI: 10.3390/ijerph17124566. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32599962/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in 61 people, a week of abstinence went with higher well-being and connectedness and lower fear of missing out and phone use. What it does not show: a controlled comparison; there was no control group.
  3. Grüning, D. J., Riedel, F., & Lorenz-Spreen, P. (2023). Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 120(8), e2213114120. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2213114120. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36795756/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a six-week field experiment (N = 280) with a short pause before opening chosen apps. What it does not show: a control group in the field part (none described in the abstract). (Title left out because it contains the app's brand name.)
  4. Olson, J. A., Sandra, D. A., Chmoulevitch, D., Raz, A., & Veissiere, S. P. L. (2022). A nudge-based intervention to reduce problematic smartphone use: Randomised controlled trial. International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction, 1-23. DOI: 10.1007/s11469-022-00826-w. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35600564/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a ten-strategy package lowered problematic use and screen time and improved sleep quality versus a monitoring control (N = 70). What it does not show: which strategy mattered.
  5. Hunt, M. G., Marx, R., Lipson, C., & Young, J. (2018). No more FOMO: Limiting social media decreases loneliness and depression. Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology. DOI: 10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751. Read: abstract (Semantic Scholar record; the publisher page refused automated access). Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: both groups improved on some measures over baseline, which the authors read as a benefit of self-monitoring. What it does not show: effects beyond three weeks. Volume and pages not checked, so not given.
