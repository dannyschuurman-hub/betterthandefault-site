Attention Residue: Why Switching Tasks Leaves Your Mind Behind

Dit artikel is alleen in het Engels beschikbaar. Hieronder staat een korte samenvatting in het Nederlands. Dat is geen vertaling van het hele artikel. Geen medisch advies.

Samenvatting

Als je aan een nieuwe taak begint, denk je soms nog aan de vorige. Dat heet aandachtsresidu. In experimenten presteerden mensen die aan de vorige taak bleven denken minder goed op de volgende. Een telefoonmelding kon de aandacht al verstoren. In ander onderzoek hielp het om kort op te schrijven hoe je een onderbroken taak later weer zou oppakken. De focussprint krijgt het label ‘Our advice, not proven’.

Bronnen

De bronnen staan in het Engels, zoals in het artikel. We vermelden alleen studies die we zelf hebben geopend en gelezen.

  1. Leroy, S. (2009). Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 109(2), 168-181. DOI: 10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002 (DOI taken from the Crossref record; the record page at ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v109y2009i2p168-181.html was opened and read; the publisher page is subscriber-only). Not listed in PubMed. Read: abstract, paraphrased. What it shows: in two experiments, people who did not stop thinking about a previous task performed worse on the next one, and time pressure on the first task helped them disengage. What it does not show: the abstract gives no sample size, and the tasks were experimental work tasks.
  2. Leroy, S., & Glomb, T. M. (2018). Tasks interrupted: How anticipating time pressure on resumption of an interrupted task causes attention residue and low performance on interrupting tasks and how a "ready-to-resume" plan mitigates the effects. Organization Science. DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2017.1184. Read: abstract (via the Semantic Scholar record). What it shows: across four studies, anticipated time pressure on resuming an interrupted task went with attention residue and weaker performance on the interrupting task, and a brief ready-to-resume plan reduced it. What it does not show: sample sizes (not in the abstract) or effects in everyday phone use.
  3. Stothart, C., Mitchum, A., & Yehnert, C. (2015). The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance. DOI: 10.1037/xhp0000100. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26121498/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: notifications alone disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task, by an amount comparable to active phone use. What it does not show: the abstract gives no sample size, and it is a lab task, not real-life work.
