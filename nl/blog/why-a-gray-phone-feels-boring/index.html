Why a Gray Phone Feels Boring (and Whether That Is the Point)

Dit artikel is in het Engels. Hieronder staat een korte Nederlandse samenvatting. Het is geen vertaling van het hele artikel en geen medisch advies.

Samenvatting

Studies laten zien dat een grijs scherm, of een pakket met een grijs scherm erin, de gemeten schermtijd verlaagt. Of je je er beter door voelt is gemengd, en of het komt doordat het scherm saai wordt, is niet getest. Better Than Default geeft "Backed by research", alleen voor minder schermtijd.

Bronnen

De bronnen staan in het Engels, zoals in het artikel. We vermelden alleen studies die we zelf hebben geopend en gelezen.

  1. Zimmermann, L., & Sobolev, M. (2023). Digital strategies for screen time reduction: A randomized field experiment. Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking, 26(1), 42-49. DOI: 10.1089/cyber.2022.0027. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36577008/. Read: abstract (via PubMed record and Europe PMC). What it shows: grayscale cut objectively measured screen time versus monitoring only (N = 112). What it does not show: any immediate effect on well-being or academic performance.
  2. Dekker, C. A., & Baumgartner, S. (2023). Is life brighter when your phone is not? The efficacy of a grayscale smartphone intervention addressing digital well-being. Mobile Media & Communication. DOI: 10.1177/20501579231212062. Read: abstract (Semantic Scholar record; publisher page refused access). What it shows: with 84 participants, about 20 minutes less daily screen time, unlocks unchanged. What it does not show: productivity or sleep effects, or a separate control group.
  3. Wickord, L.-C., & Quaiser-Pohl, C. (2023). Suffering from problematic smartphone use? Why not use grayscale setting as an intervention! An experimental study. Computers in Human Behavior Reports, 10, 100294. DOI: 10.1016/j.chbr.2023.100294. Read: abstract (university repository page), summarised on opening; metadata via Crossref. Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: a repeated-measures experiment, 240 participants in the first questionnaire and 92 in the second, with reduced usage time after at least 24 hours of grayscale. What it does not show: effects beyond about a day, or a control group.
  4. Olson, J. A., Sandra, D. A., Chmoulevitch, D., Raz, A., & Veissiere, S. P. L. (2022). A nudge-based intervention to reduce problematic smartphone use: Randomised controlled trial. International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction, 1-23. DOI: 10.1007/s11469-022-00826-w. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35600564/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a bundle of ten strategies, including grayscale, lowered problematic use and screen time against a monitoring control (N = 70). What it does not show: which strategy did the work.
  5. Westgate, E. C., & Wilson, T. D. (2018). Boring thoughts and bored minds: The MAC model of boredom and cognitive engagement. Psychological Review, 125(5), 689-713. DOI: 10.1037/rev0000097. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29963873/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a theory of boredom as a sign of unsuccessful attentional engagement. What it does not show: anything about grayscale screens.
