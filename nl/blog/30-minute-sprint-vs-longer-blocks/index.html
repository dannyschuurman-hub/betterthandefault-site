The 30-Minute Sprint vs. Longer Blocks: What Studies on Breaks Can and Cannot Tell You

Dit artikel is alleen in het Engels beschikbaar. Hieronder staat een korte samenvatting in het Nederlands. Dat is geen vertaling van het hele artikel. Geen medisch advies.

Samenvatting

We vonden geen studie die blokken van 30, 60 en 90 minuten vergelijkt bij werk dat veel concentratie vraagt. In één studie voelden studenten zich beter bij geplande pauzes dan bij zelfgekozen pauzes. Een overzichtsstudie vond kleine effecten van korte pauzes op energie en vermoeidheid. De onderzoekers opperen dat veeleisend werk om langere pauzes kan vragen. De sprint krijgt het label ‘Our advice, not proven’. ‘Real breaks, not scrolling’ krijgt ‘Small study’.

Bronnen

De bronnen staan in het Engels, zoals in het artikel. We vermelden alleen studies die we zelf hebben geopend en gelezen.

  1. Biwer, F., Wiradhany, W., Oude Egbrink, M. G. A., & de Bruin, A. B. H. (2023). British Journal of Educational Psychology, 93(Suppl 2), 353-367. DOI: 10.1111/bjep.12593. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36859717/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: 87 students, one day; planned breaks (6 minutes after 24, or 3 minutes after 12) went with better mood and similar task completion in shorter time than self-regulated breaks. What it does not show: effects over weeks, other groups, or blocks of 60 or 90 minutes. (Title left out because it contains a technique's brand name.)
  2. Albulescu, P., Macsinga, I., Rusu, A., Sulea, C., Bodnaru, A., & Tulbure, B. T. (2022). "Give me a break!" A systematic review and meta-analysis on the efficacy of micro-breaks for increasing well-being and performance. PLoS ONE, 17(8), e0272460. DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0272460. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36044424/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: 22 samples, N = 2,335; small effects on vigor and fatigue; performance effect not significant; longer breaks gave a bigger performance boost. What it does not show: an ideal break length.
  3. Blasche, G., Szabo, B., Wagner-Menghin, M., Ekmekcioglu, C., & Gollner, E. (2018). Comparison of rest-break interventions during a mentally demanding task. Stress and Health, 34(5), 629-638. DOI: 10.1002/smi.2830. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30113771/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: with 66 students in two four-hour classes, breaks with exercise or relaxation did more for vigor and fatigue than an unstructured break, 20 minutes later. What it does not show: effects on performance, or on other groups.
  4. Grobelny, J., Glinka, M., & Chirkowska-Smolak, T. (2024). The impact of hedonic social media use during microbreaks on employee resources recovery. Scientific Reports, 14, 21603. DOI: 10.1038/s41598-024-72825-x. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39284901/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in a pre-registered experiment with 308 employees, algorithmic video microbreaks gave partial recovery, and nature did better. What it does not show: long-term effects.
  5. Ariga, A., & Lleras, A. (2011). Brief and rare mental "breaks" keep you focused: Deactivation and reactivation of task goals preempt vigilance decrements. Cognition, 118(3), 439-443. DOI: 10.1016/j.cognition.2010.12.007. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21211793/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: in a lab vigilance task, brief switches away from the main goal averted the usual performance decline. What it does not show: sample size (not in the abstract), or effects in real work.
