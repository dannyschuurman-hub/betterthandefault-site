Single-Tasking in Practice: One Thing on the Screen, One Thing in Your Head

Dit artikel is in het Engels. Hieronder staat een korte Nederlandse samenvatting. Het is geen vertaling van het hele artikel en geen medisch advies.

Samenvatting

Wisselen tussen taken kost aantoonbaar tijd in laboproeven, en een onafgemaakte taak houdt aandacht vast; een korte notitie over hoe je terugkeert kan dat verkleinen. Of zwaar multitasken je focus kapotmaakt is niet vastgesteld, want de richting van het verband is onbekend. Eén ding tegelijk is daarom "Our advice, not proven", met meldingen in bundels als onderbouwd extraatje.

Bronnen

De bronnen staan in het Engels, zoals in het artikel. We vermelden alleen studies die we zelf hebben geopend en gelezen.

  1. Rubinstein, J. S., Meyer, D. E., & Evans, J. E. (2001). Executive control of cognitive processes in task switching. Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 27(4), 763-797. DOI: 10.1037/0096-1523.27.4.763. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11518143/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: four experiments; task alternation produced switching-time costs, larger with complex rules and smaller with cues. What it does not show: sample sizes (not in the abstract), or real-world costs.
  2. Leroy, S. (2009). Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks. Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 109(2), 168-181. DOI: 10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002. Read: abstract (RePEc page), summarised on opening. Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: in two experiments, an unfinished task left attention behind. What it does not show: sample size (not in the abstract).
  3. Leroy, S., & Glomb, T. M. (2018). Tasks interrupted: How anticipating time pressure on resumption of an interrupted task causes attention residue and low performance on interrupting tasks and how a "ready-to-resume" plan mitigates the effects. Organization Science, 29, 380-397. DOI: 10.1287/orsc.2017.1184. Read: abstract (Semantic Scholar record). Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: across four studies, a ready-to-resume plan reduced attention residue. What it does not show: sample sizes (not in the abstract).
  4. Ophir, E., Nass, C., & Wagner, A. D. (2009). Cognitive control in media multitaskers. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 106(37), 15583-15587. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.0903620106. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19706386/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: heavy media multitaskers were more susceptible to interference and worse at task switching. What it does not show: cause and effect.
  5. Uncapher, M. R., & Wagner, A. D. (2018). Minds and brains of media multitaskers: Current findings and future directions. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 115(40), 9889-9896. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1611612115. PubMed: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30275312/. Read: abstract (via Europe PMC). What it shows: a review with mixed findings, many studies finding no differences. What it does not show: whether multitasking causes the differences.
  6. Fitz, N., Kushlev, K., Jagannathan, R., Lewis, T., Paliwal, D., & Ariely, D. (2019). Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being. Computers in Human Behavior, 101, 84-94. DOI: 10.1016/j.chb.2019.07.016. Read: full text (open PDF copy). Not listed in PubMed. What it shows: in a randomised field experiment (N = 237, two weeks), batching three times a day went with feeling more attentive, in a better mood and more in control. What it does not show: effects beyond two weeks or for other groups; outcomes were mostly self-reported.
